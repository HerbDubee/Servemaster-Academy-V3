Chapter 5 — A Day in the City

She woke to the sound of the street.

Not an alarm. Not a prep call. Just Buenos Aires on a Sunday in February — a bus somewhere several blocks away, a dog, the distant rhythm of someone else's cumbia bleeding out of a window and dissolving before it reached her. She lay still and let it happen.

The light on the far wall was doing something. She watched it for a moment without identifying what. Luca's side of the bed was empty, but she could hear him — the specific quiet of someone moving carefully through a kitchen so as not to wake anyone. The coffee smell arrived before she decided whether to get up.

The apartment had taken on its shared shape in the weeks since she'd stopped treating it as temporary. Not planned. Just accumulated: his jacket on the hook that had been empty, two mugs on the counter where there'd been one, her book face-down on the side table beside his. The detritus of two people who had stopped performing the beginning of something.

She got up. Found the second mug already waiting.

"Market?" he said. Not looking up from the window, where he was watching something below.

"Give me twenty minutes."

Palermo Viejo on a Sunday had a texture different from the rest of the week. Less traffic meant you could hear the city differently — pigeons, a child somewhere, the scrape of a chair being set out by a café that was just opening. The jacarandas were past their peak but still carrying enough purple that the light through them fell soft and slightly unreal.

They walked without hurrying. That was new. They were people who moved through spaces with purpose — checked sightlines, clocked exits, noted who needed something before they knew it themselves. Today neither of them was doing that. She noticed herself not doing it and said nothing.

The market had already warmed up by the time they arrived. Stalls under canvas awnings, a panadería at the corner exhaling something buttery into the street. A dog was asleep in a doorway on the shaded side, one paw over its nose, indifferent to the foot traffic. Sofia stopped to look at it. Luca waited. She took a photo.

"Working?" he said.

"No." She pocketed her phone. "Just a dog."

The market was the kind that didn't perform itself — no signage announcing artisanal anything, just vendors who had been there long enough to stop explaining what they sold. Sofia stopped at a fruit stall and bought figs, the small dark ones, and a wedge of a local cheese she didn't know the name of but the vendor had pressed into her hand as a sample and she'd made a noise that apparently settled the matter. Two blocks further, Luca disappeared into a bakery and came back with a loaf still warm enough that she could feel the heat through the paper bag when he handed it to her.

They ate standing at a counter at the edge of the market. A juice vendor had set out a few stools. The city moved around them — a couple with a stroller, two old men in an argument that was clearly ongoing and comfortable, a teenager on a bicycle carrying something precarious.

Luca tore off bread. She opened the cheese. He reached into her bag for a fig without asking.

She didn't comment. He ate it. She ate one. The bread was good.

The Recoleta Cemetery in the afternoon was a different kind of quiet. Not the quiet of an empty place — there were tourists, a few tour groups, a family who seemed to be visiting someone specific and were taking it seriously. But the marble absorbed sound differently than glass and concrete. You became slightly quieter in it without deciding to.

The mausoleums were extraordinary in the way that things built to outlast their builders often were: overdone, sincere, occasionally breathtaking. Angels in various states of grief. Ornate ironwork. Inscriptions that assumed a reader who would arrive with sufficient reverence. The cats were everywhere — municipal, unhurried, occupying sunlit corners with the authority of things that had been somewhere longer than anyone present.

Sofia had her camera out. Not the phone — the actual camera she rarely brought to work. She was photographing the light, specifically: the way it came through the canopy of trees at the northeast corner and hit the white marble at a low angle, creating shadows that made the stonework look carved from the inside.

Luca watched her more than he watched the cemetery.

"There's something instructive about this," she said, not looking up from the viewfinder.

"The dead?"

"The ambition." She adjusted the focus. "Every one of these was built by someone who thought this was how you lasted. A room that didn't serve anyone. A marble room that couldn't be rearranged." She lowered the camera. "And here we are walking through it on a Sunday like it's a park."

"We do that with hotels too," he said. "The old ones. The ones built in 1920 by someone who thought they were building something eternal." He looked up at an angel that was at least three meters tall, expression unreadable. "Some of them made it."

"The ones that kept working," she said.

"The ones that figured out what they were actually for."

She raised the camera again. He looked at the angel. Neither of them said anything else about it.

She found the one with the missing hand near the back, in a row of similar figures that had weathered differently from each other — some pristine, some gone green, one that had cracked along the face in a way that looked almost deliberate. This one was reaching. The raised arm ended cleanly at the wrist, as though the hand had simply decided to be elsewhere.

She photographed it. Held out the screen so he could see.

He looked at it. She looked at it. They kept walking.

The café had tables outside under a canvas awning that had once been yellow and was now the color of old cream. They sat in the shade and ordered a bottle of Malbec that arrived with two glasses and a small dish of olives no one had asked for.

The afternoon had that specific February quality — the heat flattening slightly after two o'clock, the shadows lengthening without cooling anything down. There was no urgency in the air. The café understood this. Their waiter appeared when they needed him and not otherwise.

"Worst city," Luca said, apropos of nothing. They'd been talking about cities — where they'd worked, what each one felt like from the service side. "Go."

"Professionally or personally?"

"Your choice."

She thought about it. "Dubai. Not the city — the city was fine. A specific property. The kind of management that read about hospitality somewhere and decided the main thing was uniforms." She picked up her glass. "We had a new collar specification. It had to be hand-measured every morning. By a designated person."

"Who was the designated person?"

"It rotated. Which made it worse."

He laughed — not politely. She took a drink.

"Mine was a resort in the Maldives," he said. "Six months. The guests were fine. The other staff were fine. The GM had a philosophy."

"What kind of philosophy?"

"The kind that got printed on laminated cards." He turned his glass by the stem. "There were eleven of them. Hung in every back-of-house corridor. You couldn't walk to the laundry without encountering three axioms."

"What were they?"

"I've blocked most of them. One was —" he paused, apparently excavating — " 'Excellence is not a destination. It is a direction.' "

"That's not even grammatically coherent."

"There was a quiz," he said. "Monthly."

She put her glass down. "You're making that up."

"I'm not."

"A quiz."

"Multiple choice."

She looked at him. He was not smiling in a way that meant he absolutely was not making it up. She started laughing and stopped herself and started again. He watched this happen with evident satisfaction.

"What was the prize for passing?" she managed.

"There was no prize. There were consequences for failing."

"What consequences?"

"Additional laminated cards. In your room."

The Malbec was good — not performing anything, just wine with some depth to it. They ordered another glass each when the bottle ran low and did not hurry. She told him about her first week in Rome, the miscommunication that had resulted in her attempting to explain to a guest in Italian that his morning suit was not, in fact, missing, and what had actually happened to it. He told her about a wedding in Edinburgh that had involved a live piper, three flights of stairs, and a cake that in retrospect should have been transported differently.

At some point her hand was on the table. His came to rest near it. Not quite touching. Then touching.

They both felt it — that small specific shift of attention. She didn't move her hand. He didn't move his. The conversation resumed. Two people who were new enough to each other that a hand on a table still registered, still meant something they hadn't finished cataloguing.

The city turned gold and then pink and then the deeper blue of a February evening that didn't want to cool down. They walked back through streets that were quieter than morning without being empty — a Sunday gathering itself in, families at restaurant tables visible through glass, the smell of grilling from somewhere.

The apartment: she found pasta in the cupboard and made it simply, with olive oil and garlic and some of the cheese from the market, roughly grated. He opened another bottle — a different one, something lighter. They ate on the balcony, which was small enough that their knees almost touched across the little table.

Below them the city was coming on. Window lights, headlights, the glow of a restaurant sign. The neighborhood they were in was quiet by Buenos Aires standards, which was not quiet, but it was the comfortable noise of a place living in itself rather than performing.

They talked less over dinner. Not from the absence of things to say — from the presence of enough space to not say them. She ate. He refilled her glass. The cats from the cemetery came back to her for a moment, their occupying ease, their complete indifference to history.

She cleared the plates. He didn't move from the balcony. When she came back she stood at the railing, and the city was there, doing what cities do in the evening — offering its lit distances, its suggestions of other lives in other lit windows.

He came to stand behind her. She leaned back.

They stayed like that for a while. She thought about how she'd spent most of the last year organizing her life around transition points. End of contract. Next placement. The specific competence she'd developed at arriving and departing, at establishing herself quickly in a new place because quick establishment was what the work required. She had become very good at it. She hadn't entirely noticed when it had stopped being only a professional skill.

"I'm scared," she said. Not dramatically. The city was still there. "Of how much I want this."

He didn't speak immediately. She felt him settle slightly — not pulling away, not pushing forward. Just landing.

"Not just you," she said. "This. Being in the same city. The idea of —" she stopped. Looked for the honest version. "I'm already making contingency plans. I'm doing it right now, underneath everything else. Already figuring out how it ends."

It wasn't self-pity. She recognized that clearly enough. It was something more structural — the training toward impermanence that the industry had given her without asking, the reflex she'd developed over years of beautiful temporary things. Be present. Be excellent. Be ready to leave.

She had gotten very good at being ready to leave.

"I know," he said.

"It's a strange thing to get good at."

"Yes."

She waited. He said: "I keep thinking about how I'll explain this —" a pause, finding the shape of it — "not to other people. To myself. In a year. Or three. Whatever version of this there is." He set his hands on the railing beside hers. "I've been doing that long enough that it's automatic. The pre-narrative. The way to carry it when it's over."

She turned her head slightly. He was looking at the city.

"I'm more scared of going back to how it was before," he said. "Than of whatever happens if we stay."

She thought about that. The city offered its lights. A bus somewhere. Someone's music, briefly, then gone.

"Okay," she said.

He waited.

"Then we keep going."

Not resolution. Just the decision made plainly, the way she made most decisions — present tense, forward-facing, without asking for guarantees that didn't exist. She knew how to carry uncertainty. She had been carrying it for years. What she was deciding now was to carry it toward something rather than away.

She felt him breathe. His hand moved to cover hers on the railing.

They stood there until the air shifted — one of those small Buenos Aires breezes that arrived after ten with the faint salt suggestion of something distant. She shivered once. He didn't comment on it. They went inside.

The wine sat forgotten on the counter. Neither of them went back for it.

Outside, the city went on doing what it did — its lights, its distances, its accumulated noise of lives running in parallel, largely indifferent to any one of them, and in that indifference something very close to permission.
