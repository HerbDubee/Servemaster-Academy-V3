Chapter 10 — Barcelona Farewell

The Cordon Bleu exam had been on a Friday.

She'd prepared in the way she prepared everything — systematically, and then by ignoring the system. The systematic part: two weeks of working through every practical element she'd been assessed on, the mise en place protocols, the classical service sequences, the wine pairing frameworks. The ignoring-the-system part: the night before, she'd gone to Quimet & Quimet, had two of whatever Joaquim was most pleased with that evening, and spent an hour talking to him about something entirely unrelated to service — his grandfather's time in the merchant navy, the tinned fish from those years that had become a family trade — and she'd gone home and slept without reviewing her notes.

She passed with the highest mark in her cohort.

The instructor had said, afterward, in the brief individual feedback session: You don't serve food. You create a room. That will take you somewhere.

She'd written it in her notebook and then, below it, written: This is the last formal assessment. Everything from here is the actual thing.

There were three days before the train to London. She'd planned them as a proper farewell — the city she'd spent three months treating as a classroom deserved a goodbye in which she wasn't learning anything, just being in it.

The first day she walked the Gothic Quarter in full tourist mode: the cathedral, the Roman walls, the little squares where the light fell differently in each one, the narrow streets where you couldn't see the sky but could hear it. She let herself be lost twice, which she'd been too purposeful to allow herself before. The lost streets were the better ones — quieter, more residential, laundry on lines, a child chasing a dog, an elderly couple moving at the pace of people who had successfully navigated a very long time together.

She thought about that couple for a while after she'd passed them. The ease of two people who'd had forty years to learn how to move through space together, the particular grammar of a long partnership. She filed it away in the way she filed things she didn't have a framework for yet — as something true that she hadn't found the right category for.

The second day she went to the Picasso Museum, which she'd been intending to do since week two and had not managed until now. She stood for a long time in front of the Las Meninas series — the fifty-eight variations on Velázquez, Picasso's extended conversation with a painting, the way he took a thing apart and showed you all the ways it could be put back together differently. She found it unexpectedly moving. Not because of the virtuosity — the virtuosity was obvious — but because of the persistence. Fifty-eight versions. The insistence that you could keep finding new angles on the same true thing.

She wrote in the museum café: The thing you practice is not the product. The thing you practice produces the understanding that makes the product possible. Picasso isn't trying to out-Velázquez Velázquez. He's trying to understand painting. The service is the same. You're not trying to execute the technique. You're trying to understand hospitality. The technique is just the current form that takes.

She read it back and thought: I would not have written this three months ago. I am not the same person who arrived in Barcelona.

She thought about what she'd been three months ago, and six months ago, and what she'd been when she'd folded the Cordon Bleu letter into the backpack. Not worse. Just earlier. Just smaller in the good direction — smaller because more of the knowledge hadn't come in yet. She was larger now in the sense that counts, which is the sense of understanding. Not done. Never done. Just — more.

The third day she went to Quimet for the last time.

She arrived at eleven, before the official opening, because Joaquim had told her to. He opened the door himself, which was unusual, and handed her a coffee and gestured at a stool.

"Nos quedamos un rato," he said. We stay for a bit.

They sat for an hour. He told her about the bar's history — his grandfather in 1914, the original location, the move to this one in the forties, the years when things had been hard and the bar was the constant. She told him about Florence and the blood orange and the boy who needed a pasta tornado. He laughed at the tornado story.

"Lo mismo," he said. It's the same thing. "The child doesn't need the dish to be good. He needs to feel that someone saw what he needed."

"Yes," she said. "That's exactly it."

"Every guest is the child." He poured a small measure of something fortified, something she couldn't identify, and set it between them on the bar. "In some way. Some need it more obviously."

"How do you know how obvious it is?"

He tilted his head in a way that meant: you look. Just look. "You come from restaurants," he said. "Tables. Distance. Here, no distance. I'm here." He touched the bar. "They're there. The bar is not a barrier. It's the thing we're both leaning on."

She'd thought about this for three days before she wrote it down, because it was the kind of thing that required some time to settle. In her notebook, on the train to London, she finally wrote it: The bar is not a barrier. It's the thing we're both leaning on. The service position is not distance. It's the shared structure. You're both resting your weight on the same surface.

She also wrote, because she'd been thinking about it since the Gothic Quarter: The grammar of a long partnership. Two people who've learned to move through space together. What's the professional version of that? The table that knows you're coming before you arrive, the read that happens faster because the relationship is longer, the room that trusts you enough to give you the information you need. Can that be built in an evening? Elena could. Marco could. Joaquim does it in the first ten minutes at the bar.

And then, without planning to, she wrote: I wonder if the notebook is still being used. The dense handwriting. The floor plan on the page I saw for half a second on the bridge.

She left that line in. She didn't cross it out this time.

London next. Ikoyi — she'd gotten the stage through a recommendation from Marta, a single email that had apparently been sufficient, which told her something about Marta's reputation in the circuit. She'd read about Ikoyi. She'd read Jeremy's interviews. She knew the room was going to ask something different from her than Disfrutar had, than Florence had, than any of the previous rooms.

She was ready. Or more precisely: she was decided. Which she now understood was the available alternative to ready.

Se lanza.

She watched Spain give way through the window, the landscape flattening toward the coast, the Mediterranean glinting for a last moment before the train curved north. She'd arrived in Barcelona knowing her craft academically and the city not at all. She was leaving with Joaquim's bar and the Picasso museum and a Cordon Bleu certificate and an understanding of millimeter work that lived in her hands now and would never leave.

She thought about all the rooms she'd learned in and felt, for the first time, not like a student moving from classroom to classroom but like a person building something. A thing that was accumulating. A practice that was becoming a self.

The train moved through France.

She closed the notebook, settled into the seat, and slept without dreaming, which was the sleep of someone who has done what they came to do.
