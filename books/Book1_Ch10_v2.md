Chapter 10 — Barcelona Farewell

She woke at five-fifteen without an alarm, which was how she knew she was nervous. The exam didn't start until nine. She lay in the gray El Born light and listened to the building breathe — a neighbor's radio, distant and tinny, the creak of pipes — and tried to run through the Priorat's soil profile one more time, but her mind kept jumping to the mechanics of the decant instead. The weight of the bottle. The slow tilt. She'd practiced it so many times the gesture had moved somewhere below conscious recall, which was either reassuring or a reason to panic, and she genuinely couldn't decide which.

She got up and made coffee and stood at the window in her undershirt watching the laundry line sway below. Someone's blue shirt. The red dress that was always there. She had never once seen anyone collect or hang that dress; it had appeared in November and she had taken it as a fixed feature of Barcelona, immovable as the gargoyles.

Look before you pour. Elena's voice, not requested. It was never about the glass. She put on her uniform.

The exam room smelled of beeswax and something anxious — two dozen bodies in pressed black uniforms trying not to sweat through their shirts. Sofia stood at station five and looked at her setup without touching anything. The Rioja in its cradle. The Priorat in the decanting basket, already prepped from the earlier unseen stage, a slow amber bloom visible in the neck if you caught the light right. Linen so precisely folded it looked architectural. The crystal stemware threw back small, exact flashes from the recessed track lighting above.

She breathed. Three seconds of stillness, the way Elena had pressed into her until it was structural. Her hands were very still at her sides.

At the station next to her, she could hear Carlos — sharp, capable Carlos who'd scored highest in every mock — doing a barely audible mutter-review of the Rioja vintage chart. She did not look at him. She looked at the crystal.

Estación cinco, comience.

Her hands moved before her thoughts caught up with them.

The Rioja came out of its cradle cleanly — label angled toward the host position, grip invisible, no telegraphing. She heard her own voice begin: black cherry, notes of vanilla oak, the tannins structured to cut the fat of the jamón — and it surprised her slightly, the way her voice sounded: not performed, just informative, the way you'd tell someone interesting about something on a walk. The first pour went clockwise and settled in the glass without a sound. The finish was the small wrist-turn, the linen catching the last drop as it threatened the neck. She stepped back. The instructor playing host — Professor Molina, who she'd had for three semesters and who wore his

skepticism like a good overcoat — tilted the glass, swirled, looked up at her with that expression she still couldn't read. Not approving. Not withholding. Just waiting.

Exquisite balance, he said.

She kept her face neutral. Inside, something very small unclenched — not the big knot, just one outer cord of it, loosening by a fraction.

Then the questions, coming fast. Vintage variances — she'd drilled these until they stopped feeling like dates and started feeling like weather. Soil profiles: the garnacha on Priorat's steep llicorella slate, why the low yields concentrated the fruit, why that concentration needed exactly the kind of decanting she was about to perform. She answered while she moved because she'd learned to talk and work at the same time on nights when Quimet was at capacity and someone needed narration and a bomba simultaneously.

The Priorat decant was where she slowed.

She'd watched her own hands do this a hundred times in the El Born flat, bottle tilted at thirty-five degrees over the candle, the sediment banking toward the punt. She'd done it until her forearms ached. Now, under the recessed lights with Molina's eyes tracking every millimeter, she found the angle by feel — not by looking at the bottle but by looking at the candle flame beyond it, watching for the first shadow of sediment moving in the neck. The wine moved in one slow, continuous thread into the decanter, amber and crimson layering without turbulence. Her wrist held the angle for forty seconds. Fifty. The sediment shadow appeared at the shoulder and she stopped, placed the bottle, and took a single controlled breath through her nose.

The fruit needs air, she said. Fifteen minutes minimum. I'd give a guest something to talk about while we wait.

Molina made a note. He did not change his expression, but she caught it — a micro-adjustment at the corner of his mouth that she recognized, from six months at Quimet, as the face of someone who'd seen something they hadn't expected to see. She filed it quietly.

The transfer to the octopus pairing went clean. The food questions were almost restful after the wine mechanics, facts settling into place with the solidity of things rehearsed past fear. By the end she wasn't performing. She was just working. And somewhere in the middle of the octopus narration she felt the big knot release — not with drama, just a slow deflation, like letting a breath out she'd been holding since five-fifteen.

Her score appeared on the screen.

Ninety-eight percent. Distinction.

The applause was subdued and entirely genuine, the kind that comes from a room full of people who understand exactly how hard the thing you just did was. She accepted it the way you accept rain — briefly, gratefully, without making it mean too much. Molina caught her eye as the room dispersed and gave one nod, the same grade as Quim's grunted buen salvamento — small, specific, worth more

than he knew.

That evening the Gothic Quarter was a different city.

Rain had come in off the sea around nine and stayed, and the medieval alleys darkened and shone and smelled of wet stone and something frying in olive oil somewhere up around the next turn. Sofia walked without a destination, boots finding the uneven cobblestones by memory, rain tapping her hood in a rhythm that was almost music. Overhead, laundry lines sagged under wet sheets. A cat watched from a doorway, thoroughly dry, thoroughly unimpressed.

She'd lived inside this neighborhood for six months and she still didn't know all of it. That was the thing about Barcelona — you thought you had it and then it turned a corner on you. New bar in a wall you'd passed a hundred times. A staircase going somewhere you hadn't noticed. The sudden sound of a guitar from behind a closed shutter, three bars of something beautiful, then nothing. Under a gargoyle's drip she stopped and let the city wash over her.

She'd thought she would feel triumphant. She didn't, exactly. The exam score was real and she was glad of it, but it sat at some distance from this feeling, which was more complicated. She was leaving in two days and the rain was doing something to the streets — turning the paving stones to mirrors, making the city look like its own reflection — and she became aware of things she hadn't registered consciously all year. The specific quality of the light at this hour, orange from the old fixtures, softened by wet air. The way the sound of Spanish and Catalan mixed in the stairwells and alleyways, a chord she'd stopped hearing because it had become background, and now that she was leaving she could hear it again. The jamón vendor's voice from the covered market three alleys over, already shutting down for the night. She would not eat jamón like that anywhere she was going. She thought about Florence. The girl who'd stepped off the train at Santa Maria Novella with a too-heavy backpack and a very specific terror of dropping a tray. How close that girl had been to quitting after the first week. The night she'd sat on her bunk in Signora Rossi's spare room and written out two versions of a text to her mother — one that said I'm staying, one that said I think I made a mistake — and then deleted both and gone to sleep. She'd never told anyone how close it had been. Some truths you just carry.

Barcelona had been different from the start — faster, louder, less polished than Florence's marble surfaces, but alive in a way that got under your skin before you noticed it happening. What she hadn't expected was how much she'd miss the specific texture of this aliveness. Not the headline version — not the Sagrada Família, not the vermouth bars mentioned in every travel piece — but the small things she'd stopped noticing. The municipal pool two blocks from the flat where she'd swum early morning laps in the last of autumn, the water almost too cold, the lane ropes slapping in the draft from the door. The bakery on the corner where the woman who worked mornings knew her coffee order and handed it over without a word, and how that small daily wordlessness had felt like being known. The smell of the Mediterranean when the wind came from the right direction, salt and diesel and something floral she'd never identified.

She was not the same person who'd arrived here. She was starting to wonder what got left behind as

well as what you took.

Her phone buzzed in her coat pocket. Elena from class: London intensive this weekend — advanced hospitality seminar at Le Cordon Bleu London. Spots filling fast. You in?

She read it standing under the archway while water sheeted off stone above her. The sensible answer involved her bank balance, which was not good, and her sleep deficit, which was worse. But there was something in the message that arrived before the sensible answer could make its case — not excitement exactly, more like recognition. The tug of something pulling in a direction she'd already agreed to go. She typed back: Booking now.

The confirmation came while she was still standing there, rain soft on the stones. Ryanair, Friday dawn to Sunday dusk. She slipped the phone into her pocket and walked home, lighter for no logical reason, the rain still going, the city mirroring itself in every puddle underfoot. The farewell at Quimet i Quimet happened the way most good things in Barcelona happened: unexpectedly, loudly, without enough room for everyone.

Quim had opened the bar on a Friday night when it was already packed to its absolute physical limit and somehow made space for twelve more people. That was the talent. Sofia had watched him do it all year — this way of expanding whatever room existed until it became larger than it had been, not by moving anything but by making everyone lean in a little closer, talk a little louder, stop caring about the eight centimeters between their shoulder and a stranger's.

He pulled her into a hug that smelled of olives and effort, then pressed a glass of house vermouth into her hand without ceremony.

Mi americana con alma española. Said quietly, only for her. Then the crew closed around them. Javier arrived already talking, which was how Javier arrived everywhere. He had a glass of something sparkling and a grin he'd clearly been saving. "You know what I was thinking about today?" He didn't wait for an answer. "Your first Friday rush. You had eight cava splits." He held up eight fingers for the room's benefit. The room — Elena, Maria, three Le Cordon Bleu classmates, two people from the wine cert cohort — leaned in. "Eight. Balanced. Threading through a Friday-night crowd." He shook his head. "I hip-checked her. Completely her fault for being in my way." "Completely his fault," Sofia said.

"She went sideways about — what, forty degrees?" Javier looked at Maria for confirmation. "Forty-five," Maria said. She'd appeared from somewhere with a plate of montaditos and looked entirely composed, the way she always looked, even in the middle of service, as if the volume of the world was a setting she could choose not to receive. "I watched from the kitchen. I thought: we're losing eight glasses and the deposit on the tray."

"And?" Elena said.

"She came back up," Maria said simply. "Just?" She made a short, matter-of-fact gesture, a pivot

recovering into a stable base. "Like she'd been doing it for years." She looked at Sofia. "You hadn't been. You'd been doing it for four days."

"I didn't know I was allowed to drop it," Sofia said.

That got a real laugh, the full-bar kind, and she felt her throat tighten in a way she hadn't anticipated. It was the laughter as much as anything — the specific sound of people laughing at a story about you that you hadn't known they'd been carrying.

Javier's phone produced music. Someone had gotten bombas from the Barceloneta spot. The stories multiplied in the way they always did at these things: each one called up another, and each other one arrived with its own particular embarrassment or improbable grace. Maria's late-night kitchen tutorials came up — the calamar plancha at midnight, the anchovy boning by feel, the saffron identification sessions that had gone on until Sofia could smell the difference between genuine La Mancha and a certain cheaper Moroccan substitute at three arm's lengths. Elena's wine cert panic arrived as comedy now, the full story: three hours before the exam, sitting on the changing room floor, convinced she was going to fail in a specific and humiliating way she'd described in precise hypothetical detail, and Sofia pouring a practice glass and making her narrate it out loud until the panic passed through description and out the other side.

Near midnight, Maria found her for a moment at the edge of the bar, away from the main current. "Disfrutar was watching you," Maria said. She said it the way she said everything — without inflection, just information. "From the third shift. One of Oriol's crew came in for lunch a month ago and watched the floor." She looked at her steadily. "You didn't notice. You were in the middle of a rush."

Sofia absorbed this.

"Did Quim know?"

"He arranged it." Maria looked back at the bar where Quim was pouring, unhurried and exact, talking to two regulars with the easy specificity of a man who remembered everything. "He doesn't say much about the people he thinks will go somewhere. He just makes sure someone sees them." She picked up her glass. "That's the compliment. The rest is just noise."

Quim clinked his glass against Sofia's near the end, when the crowd had thinned to the faithful. He didn't say anything for a long moment. Looked at her. Looked at the bar, wiped a small section of marble with the cloth he kept tucked at his hip, a gesture so habitual it was practically punctuation. "Come back when you've got three stars," he said. And turned back to the close. She walked home through the rain holding the warmth of it in her chest like something she was careful not to spill.

Packing, the next morning, in the thin early light that came through the gauzy curtains of her El Born flat.

She was precise about it — service pants, white shirts, aprons rolled tight. Le Cordon Bleu notes stacked and bound. The wine cert tucked to the top of the pile like a flag. And then, last, the sketchbook — pages thick and slightly warped from six months of Barcelona humidity. She sat on the edge of the bed and leafed through it slowly, meaning to just confirm it was all there. The Florence sketches were visible at the back: shaky, uncertain, the linework of someone who had not yet learned what to leave out. Signora Rossi's courtyard. The Arno. Elena at the pass, only half-finished because the moment had kept moving. Then Barcelona beginning — La Boqueria at dawn, the stall geometry, the tomato pyramids rearranged with such conviction by a vendor who treated the arrangement as serious art. The Gothic spires in their particular gray. The inside of Quimet i Quimet: crowd-blur and hand-motion and the light off the conservas jars. She got better, page by page. The Barcelona drawings were steadier, darker in the shadows, more willing to leave things incomplete — she'd learned that the eye fills in what the hand leaves out, which was also, she'd come to think, the first lesson of floor service.

She was flipping toward the end when she stopped.

Not because of what she'd drawn. Because of what she hadn't.

She looked back — not skimming now, counting. La Boqueria, four times. The market at Sant Antoni, twice. Quimet's bar, three times. The interior of Le Cordon Bleu's exam room. The Gothic alleyways, rain. The restaurant facades she'd walked past. The Barcelona sketches were full of exteriors, thresholds, public spaces at the moment before they filled.

Not one person drawn with a face.

She went back further. Florence: the Arno, the market, the pass, the trattoria exterior on Via della Vigna Nuova. The people were there — crowd-blur, gestures, hands — but always in motion, always turned away or caught in the middle of something that made a face unnecessary. She'd thought she was drawing people. She'd been drawing the spaces people made.

She sat with that for a moment. Outside, Barcelona was already in motion — the neighbor's radio, a motorbike, someone shouting something cheerful in Catalan from a window. She looked at the last sketch she'd done, late last night at the kitchen table: La Boqueria from memory, the jamón vendor's knife raised silver, the saffron threads like filaments of light, the crowd mid-surge. And in the center, barely visible, a small figure bent over a dropped bag while a stranger's hand came into frame to return it.

The hand. Not the face of the stranger. Just the hand, coming in from the edge of the frame. She didn't know what to make of that, not exactly. But she wrote it in the margin, small: what I'm not drawing yet. Then she closed the book and slipped it into the front pouch of the suitcase. The shuttle to El Prat was full of strangers with the glazed, forward-looking expression of people leaving somewhere. Sofia sat with her forehead against the cool glass and watched Barcelona give way to its suburbs, which gave way to the flat industrial ring of the airport approach. Through the

small oval window she watched the city tilt as they climbed — the Sagrada Família's spires reaching up as if making one last argument, the sea a gray-blue smudge beyond the port, the whole impossible place receding into mist.

She'd arrived not knowing anyone. She was leaving with a list of names she'd carry for years, probably forever. That was the thing no one told you about working in hospitality — the people. The kitchens shaped you, but the people were what you kept.

Avó's voice, somewhere underneath all of it: sweat and fire, menina.

Both. Yes. This had been both.

The clouds took the city. The plane leveled off into the white, engines steady, London waiting somewhere ahead in the gray.
