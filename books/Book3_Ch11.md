Chapter 11 — The Parting

The offer came on a Wednesday, which felt wrong. Wednesdays were ordinary days — the kind that belonged to coffee gone lukewarm on the counter and missed messages and nothing yet decided. Wednesdays were not the days that changed things.

Sofia read the email twice before she believed it. A Singapore restaurant, newly starred, serious about its next six months — the kind of place that collected talent the way certain restaurants do when they're on the edge of becoming something. The contract was six months. The money was real. The name attached to the project was a name she recognized from a stage she'd watched from the back of a symposium room two years ago, when she'd still been deciding whether she was the kind of person who went to symposiums.

She stared at the screen for a long time. Long enough that the light in the Fitzroy apartment shifted from afternoon to something softer, and the street outside produced its usual early-evening sounds — a tram, voices, a dog expressing an opinion about something.

Then she got up and found Luca.

He was at the kitchen table with the notebook open and a pen that had been dry for three days — he kept forgetting to replace it, kept reaching for it anyway. He looked up when she came in, read something in her face before she spoke, and held out his hand for her phone.

She gave it to him.

He read it without speaking. She watched him. He had a way, when he was processing something important, of going very still — not the stillness of someone withholding, but the stillness of someone who understood that words spent too early were words wasted. She had learned to trust that stillness. She didn't always like it.

What she couldn't see, watching him: the thing moving behind his eyes. The calculation that was not really a calculation but something older, something that felt, if he was honest, like standing at the edge of a decision and already knowing you were going to make the hard one. He loved her. He knew what she was. He knew what this was. And he knew — with a clarity that cost him something — that to give her any other answer was to make her smaller, and making her smaller was not love. It was the imitation of love. The kind that felt like protection and was actually fear wearing borrowed clothes.

He looked up. His face was carefully neutral.

"You should take it," he said.

Sofia felt something crack in her chest.

"I don't want to."

"I know." He set her phone on the table. "But this is the work. This is what we do."

She sat on the edge of the bed later, shoes still on, looking at nothing in particular. The room held the specific quiet of a conversation that wasn't finished.

"I'm tired of the work deciding for us," she said when he came and sat beside her.

He didn't argue with that. He took her hand instead — the way he did things sometimes, without ceremony, as if the gesture was just the obvious next step and he was only surprised she hadn't expected it.

"Then we make the decision together," he said. "You take the contract. I finish here. And we keep building. Every night. Every spare hour. We don't let the distance win this time."

Sofia looked at him. She was looking for something specific — she wasn't sure what, exactly, some tell, some tremor of ambivalence that would give her permission to say no. She didn't find it. What she found instead was steadiness, and steadiness was harder to argue with than doubt.

"Are you sure," she said. It wasn't quite a question.

"I'm sure."

She held that. The thing that was different this time was not something she had language for yet, so she didn't try. It was somewhere in the room — in the notebook on the kitchen table, in the specific weight of the last fourteen months, in the fact that they were building toward something and the building did not require them to be in the same room. The distance wasn't a pause. That was the thing. Other times it had been a pause — a held breath, a waiting for when the work was done and real life could resume. This time the work was what they were doing. The distance was just the shape the work was taking.

She didn't say any of this. Neither did he.

What he said was: "Six months is not forever."

What she said was: "No."

And then, after a moment, she stood. She felt calmer — not because anything had been resolved, but because the decision had moved from the category of things that were happening to her into the category of things she was choosing. That was a different weight. Lighter, in a way. Heavier in others.

"I'll tell them yes," she said. "But I'm not starting until I've finished here. I want every day we have left."

Luca nodded. He was still sitting on the edge of the bed, watching her. There was something in his expression she didn't have a name for and wasn't sure she wanted to name. Some feelings were more useful unnamed.

"Every day," he said.

They had six weeks. They made the most of every one.

Three of those weeks they spent entirely on the floor — Luca at Attica, Sofia at Gimlet — watching each other work whenever shifts aligned, taking notes in the margins of the notebook, building the thing. The other three they spent being people who loved each other in a city that agreed autumn was worth grieving.

The last night, they didn't go out.

Sofia had suggested it — dinner somewhere, a proper farewell, the kind of thing you could point to later as the last time — and then she'd talked herself out of it. She didn't want a last time to point to. She wanted the apartment, and Luca in it, and something unceremonious.

They cooked pasta. It was simple in the way things are simple when you're not paying attention to simplicity, just to the task in front of you — garlic in the pan, the specific smell of it, Luca handing her the wooden spoon with the short handle because that was the one she liked and he always remembered. They opened a bottle of something they'd been saving for no particular reason and now there was a reason, which was that tomorrow there would be no more particular reasons for a while.

They ate at the kitchen table. The notebook was there, pushed to one side, making room.

"The Lee family scenario," Sofia said. "I keep feeling like the second lens isn't quite right."

Luca put down his fork. "Which part."

"The part where we're asking the server to read the table for dynamic while also managing a birthday. It's two jobs."

"It's always two jobs."

"I know. But we're writing it as if it's one. Like the server is supposed to be seamless about it, and I don't think that's honest. I think we should name the split."

Luca was quiet for a moment, turning this over. "Name the split," he repeated.

"The emotional register of the table. The service mechanics. You can hold both, but they're not the same thing, and pretending they are is how you get servers who think they failed when they just did two difficult things imperfectly instead of one easy thing perfectly."

He reached for his wine. "I think you're right."

"I know."

That made him smile — the particular smile that was not amused so much as fond, the one that meant she'd said exactly what he would have said and she knew he would have said it.

"What about Priya," he said.

"She's still there. I talked to her last week." Sofia turned her glass. "She's good. She's found the part of the work that fits her, I think. The personal ritual element — she went deep on that."

"She'll be good at it."

"She already is."

They stayed on that for a moment — Priya at Gimlet, finding the work that fit her. There was something satisfying in it. The particular satisfaction of watching someone locate themselves inside a thing they'd doubted.

"Attica," Sofia said. "What are you going to do without someone watching?"

Luca looked at her. "What do you mean."

"You notice things, and then you tell me, and it becomes part of the book. That's the loop. Without the loop —"

"I'll write it down," he said. "And tell you when we call."

"Every night."

"Every night."

She looked at him across the table. The candle between them was one they'd found in a drawer when they moved in — left by whoever lived there before, unlit until tonight. Some things you just use eventually.

"I'm going to miss this kitchen," she said.

"Mm."

"The bad overhead light and the drawer that doesn't close."

"The drawer closes now. I fixed it."

"When?"

"Tuesday."

She laughed — surprised by it, which was the best kind. "You fixed the drawer two days before I leave."

"I've been meaning to."

That was true. It was also the kind of true that has a shape to it, a particular quality of things done too late and not quite too late, of small gestures that don't fix anything and aren't meant to.

After the meal they moved to the floor. Not for any reason — the sofa was right there — but the floor felt more honest, or maybe just closer to the ground in a way that felt right when something was ending. The notebook between them. The bottle within reach. The apartment settling around them with its ordinary sounds.

Sofia opened the notebook to a clean page. She held the pen for a moment. Then she wrote something — not a lens, not a lesson, not a note on service or dynamics or the architecture of a room. Something she hadn't written before, in all the months and pages. Something that belonged only to this.

She closed the notebook before he could see.

He didn't ask.

That was one of the things she loved most about him. That he understood the difference between privacy and secrecy, and he trusted her to know which this was.

They sat there until the candle was short. Then they cleaned up — together, without discussing who would do what, the way they'd learned to move in a shared kitchen — and went to bed, and held each other in the specific way of people who are practicing for absence.

Pre-dawn. The city outside was quiet in the way cities are only briefly — after the last of the night and before the first of the morning, a gap that doesn't last.

Her bag was packed. The taxi app showed a car three minutes out.

Sofia stood in the kitchen one more time. The drawer was closed. The notebook was on the table where she'd left it. She could see the shape of the apartment already becoming a thing she was leaving.

Luca was at the door.

She picked up her bag. Crossed to him.

He looked at her with that stillness — the same one from Wednesday, the same one that had always cost her something. She used to want to move it, to make him speak before he was ready. She'd stopped wanting that a long time ago. The stillness was not absence. The stillness was just how he held things that mattered.

"I'll see you in the next city," she said.

It had become their phrase. She wasn't sure when. Somewhere in the middle of this thing they were building, it had emerged — less like something invented and more like something discovered, a phrase that had always existed and simply been waiting for them to need it.

He pulled her close. Held her longer than was practical. She felt him breathe — once, slowly — and she understood that he was doing the same thing she was doing, which was memorizing.

When he spoke, his voice was quiet.

"No more almosts."

She pressed her face briefly against his shoulder.

"No more almosts."

She picked up her bag. Turned. Walked to the door and through it and down the stairs without looking back. Not because she didn't want to. Not because it would have cost her too much. But because she respected them both enough not to make this a scene. The scene was not what this was. This was something more serious than a scene. It deserved to be treated accordingly.

The car was at the curb. She got in. The driver said something quiet and she said something quiet back and then they were moving.

Upstairs, Luca stood at the window. He watched the car move through the street. He watched it reach the corner and take it — the particular way cars look when they turn, one moment present and then simply not, the space they occupied filling back in with ordinary morning.

He stood there a moment longer.

Melbourne was already beginning to be a past tense.

He turned back to the apartment. The notebook was on the table. He sat down, opened it to where she'd written last night — not to read what she'd written on that final page, because she'd closed it, and he wouldn't — but to the pages before it, the work, the lenses and lessons and late-night arguments about the right way to describe a thing.

He found the pen with the dry cartridge. Reached for it, realized, set it down.

He got up. Found a pen that worked.

Sat back down.

Turned to a clean page.

And began.
