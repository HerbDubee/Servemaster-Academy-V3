Table didn't look like much from the outside. White façade, chalkboard menu, a handful of terrace seats that looked like they'd been borrowed from someone's grandmother's garden and never returned. Twelve covers inside. No glass walls over the Seine. No sommelier station gleaming like a cockpit. Just a door, slightly ajar, and the smell of something being cooked by someone who cared about it more than was probably reasonable.

Luca chained his Vélib' to the rack on rue de Prague and stood there for a moment, hands in his pockets, feeling distinctly overdressed. His black suit — the one that made him invisible at Plénitude, that let him move through Donckele's crystal dining room like a shadow with excellent posture — felt wrong here. Like wearing a tuxedo to a bonfire.

Bruno Verjus opened the door himself. Mid-forties, wiry, forearms corded from work that involved actual soil. His apron had yesterday's beets on it, or possibly this morning's — with Verjus, the distinction was philosophical.

"Voss. From Donckele's machine." Not a question. A chin tilt toward the kitchen pass. "Ready to get dirty?"

Luca had prepared for this the way he prepared for everything: thoroughly, systematically, with notes. He'd read three interviews with Verjus, studied the seasonal menu rotation, mapped the wine list against the terroir sourcing. He had a fresh page in his notebook labelled *Table — Day 1* with columns for observations, timing, and anomalies.

None of it would help him here, and some part of him already knew that.

---

Prep at Table was controlled chaos, or possibly just chaos with good intentions. Carrots scraped by hand, herbs bruised under the flat of a blade rather than measured into prep cups, no mise en place stations — just flow, if flow meant three people moving through a space that should have held two, reaching around each other with the choreographed intimacy of people who'd been doing this long enough to stop apologizing for the elbows.

Verjus circled. He picked up a radish from the morning delivery, bit into it raw, and stood chewing with his eyes narrowed, as though the radish were making an argument he wasn't sure he agreed with.

"This one. Too bitter. Back to the farm tomorrow."

Luca reached for his notebook. Radish diameter, snap resistance, soil notes from the supplier — the variables that, at Plénitude, would have been captured in a system and flagged for kitchen review.

Verjus watched him write, then took the notebook gently from his hand, set it on the counter, and placed the radish on top of it.

"Taste it."

Luca ate the radish. It was, in fact, too bitter — a sharp, almost metallic note underneath the peppery snap, the kind of thing that would disappear under a vinaigrette but that you'd notice in Verjus's style of cooking, where ingredients arrived on the plate with nowhere to hide.

"You felt that?" Verjus asked.

"Yes."

"Good. That's the job here. Not the notebook. The mouth."

He didn't say it unkindly. He said it the way you'd correct someone's grip on a tennis racket — a small adjustment that changed everything downstream.

---

Camille, the senior server, had been at Table for five years and moved through the dining room the way rain moves through a garden — finding every surface, never forcing anything. She was in her early thirties, dark hair pulled back, laugh lines that deepened when she was genuinely amused versus the professional version she kept for guests. Luca catalogued the difference within the first hour, because that was what he did, and then wondered if cataloguing people's laugh lines made him a good observer or a strange one.

Probably both.

"You're from Plénitude?" she asked during the pre-service pause, the two of them polishing glasses at the bar.

"Three months."

"And before that?"

"Munich. Stage."

She nodded, turning a glass in the light. "Verjus will break every habit you built there. Don't fight it. Just let the room talk to you."

The room. He looked at it: oak tables scarred from years of use, mismatched chairs, an open kitchen where Verjus was already plating something with his bare hands, moving with the unhurried certainty of a man cooking dinner for people he liked. The whole space felt like someone's home that had accidentally become a restaurant and never quite noticed the transition.

At Plénitude, the room was a machine. Beautiful, precise, engineered to produce wonder at industrial scale. Here, the room was a conversation. And Luca wasn't sure he knew the language yet.

---

Service built slowly, the way a good dinner party does — quiet arrivals, coats hung on hooks that were actual hooks rather than a staffed vestiaire, Verjus greeting half the guests by name. Eight tables inside, four on the terrace. The menu had changed since yesterday because a delivery of wild strawberries had arrived from someone Verjus knew in the Dordogne, and so the entire dessert course had been rebuilt around them that afternoon.

This was, to Luca, both thrilling and deeply uncomfortable.

He handled wines. Poured a Sancerre for the couple at table five — clockwise from the right, twist at the finish, linen touch — and watched them settle into their evening. She was in silk, something celebratory in the way she held herself. He was in linen, performing relaxation the way men sometimes did when they wanted to seem at ease but weren't quite there yet. Anniversary, Luca guessed. Or the end of something difficult. The line between celebration and relief was thinner than most people realized.

By nine, the kitchen was running hot. The lamb from Auvergne — slow-roasted until it had surrendered completely, the kind of dish that made you understand why people had been cooking meat over fire for ten thousand years — was running three minutes behind. A kitchen ping. At Plénitude, three minutes was a crisis requiring intervention, wine bridges, tableside charm. Here —

Camille moved before Luca had finished processing the delay. She appeared at table five with a basket of rye bread, still warm from the oven, and a single curl of butter on a dish that might have been handmade by someone with more enthusiasm than skill. She set it down between the couple without a word. Just eye contact with the woman — held one beat longer than functional, long enough to say *I see you, you're fine, the bread is here* — and the whole table exhaled.

The woman's shoulders dropped. The man tore a chunk and the forced quality left his laugh. Three minutes passed. The lamb arrived. Nobody had noticed the gap.

Luca filed this away in a place deeper than his notebook.

---

His own moment came at table three. An older couple, both wearing the particular kind of glasses that suggested they spent their days reading things written before 1900. They'd been dissecting the wine list with the focused pleasure of people who treated dining as an intellectual exercise, which Luca usually found charming and tonight found slightly intimidating, because the Jura wines on Verjus's list were not wines he knew in his bones the way he knew Bordeaux.

The husband swirled his glass of oxidative Savagnin — golden, nutty, the kind of wine that divided rooms — and frowned. Not displeasure. Contemplation. His wife mirrored the gesture, but her glass tilted as she lifted it, stem slipping in fingers damp from the kitchen's heat bleeding into the room. A small thing. Invisible to anyone not watching.

Luca was watching.

He was there before she reached for her napkin. Fresh linen extended horizontally — not offered, just present, the way a railing appears at the edge of a staircase. A chilled replacement glass beside it, because the warmth that had made her grip slip would have changed the wine's temperature too, and a warm Savagnin lost the tension that made it interesting.

She blotted her hand, looked up, and smiled the way people smile when they've been seen without being made to feel clumsy.

"You read minds," she said.

"Only the ones worth reading," he said, low enough that it was just for her, and poured the wine into the fresh glass. Clockwise. Twist. The golden liquid settled, and the conversation at the table picked up exactly where it had paused, as though nothing had happened. Which, from their perspective, was true.

---

Debrief happened at the bar after close, Verjus wiping his hands on a rag that had served too many purposes to track. The kitchen was dark behind him, still ticking with residual heat, smelling of lamb fat and strawberries and the particular warmth of a room where something good had happened.

"You see it," Verjus said. Not a question. "The hunger before the ask. The need before the gesture. Donckele taught you to react. I want you to predict."

Luca nodded. He could feel the two systems overlapping in his mind — Plénitude's engineered precision and Table's organic instinct — like two maps of the same city drawn at different scales. Neither was wrong. But together they showed streets that didn't appear on either one alone.

---

Camille caught him at the bike rack.

"Drinks. Le Pure Café, around the corner. Verjus tradition for trial shifts."

His default response — polite decline, early run, efficiency preserved — assembled itself in his mouth and waited. But her eyes held steady, not pushing, just open, the way the best invitations simply exist without requiring an answer.

"One," he said.

Le Pure Café was an 11th arrondissement classic: zinc bar scarred from decades of elbows, mismatched stools, yellowing posters curling at the edges, the particular smell of a place where beer had been spilled in every decade since the war and no one had ever entirely mopped it up. Two locals nursed pastis at the far end. A night-shift nurse scrolled her phone near the window. Henri, the bartender — built like a man whose previous career had involved moving heavy things — drew two blondes without being asked.

They sat at the end of the bar, elbows on the cool zinc.

"Marie says you run solo at Plénitude," Camille said, foam on her upper lip. "Why?"

"Systems work cleaner alone." It came out smooth, practised. The answer he'd given every time someone got close enough to ask.

She studied him the way she'd studied the couple at table five — not analyzing, just seeing. "Verjus hates that. Says service is family. Messy, but alive."

A pause. The bar hummed around them, Henri polishing glasses with the meditative attention of a man who'd been doing it for thirty years and still found satisfaction in the transparency of clean crystal.

"My brother died last winter," Camille said. Not dramatically. The way you'd mention a fact about the weather if the weather had changed your life. "This room — Table, the team, the terrible wine Verjus opens after close — it's home now. Not a replacement. Just home."

The silence after that was the kind that asked for something in return. Not demanded. Asked.

"My Nonna," Luca said. "Positano. Lemon grove." Three words that contained a photograph, a smell, a woman's hands over his, the sound of wild trees in wind. "Five years gone."

Camille nodded. Didn't push. Sipped her beer.

They talked shop — Verjus's radish ritual, how Table's wine list was organized more by friendship than by region, the geometry of Plénitude's decanter alignment that Camille found both impressive and slightly alarming. One beer became close to two. Henri, eavesdropping with the practiced skill of a bartender who'd heard everything and forgotten nothing, leaned in.

"Heard Le Gabriel's looking. La Réserve Paris, avenue Gabriel. Jérôme Banctel. Two stars. Private money, palace hotel, the kind of room where discretion matters more than flair."

The name landed. La Réserve was not Table. La Réserve was a step up the ladder he'd been climbing since Munich — palatial scale, forty covers, suites overhead, the kind of guests who expected the world to arrange itself around their preferences and were usually right.

Camille drained her glass. "Think on it. But don't ghost us."

---

He biked home through the Marais at half past one, the streets narrow and quiet, the Seine a dark presence he could feel more than see. His apartment waited: fifteen square metres, the photograph of Nonna's lemon grove catching the streetlight through the window.

He opened his notebook. Not the observation log — the other one. The one with no columns.

*Table, Day 1. Verjus took my notebook and put a radish on it. Camille diffused a three-minute lamb delay with warm bread and one look. Professor's wife — Savagnin glass, slipping grip, replaced before she noticed. Le Pure Café: Camille lost her brother. I said Nonna's name for the first time in months.*

Below that, smaller:

*Henri mentioned a server in Florence. Brazilian. Gucci Osteria. "Reads the room like she owns it." Filed.*

He closed the notebook. Set it on the nightstand next to Nonna's photograph.

Outside, Paris settled into the particular silence it kept between two and five in the morning — not quiet, exactly, but held, like a breath between sentences in a conversation that had been going on for centuries.

Tomorrow: dawn run. The croissant. And the question of whether precision was a frame you built around the world, or a cage you built around yourself, and whether there was any difference.
