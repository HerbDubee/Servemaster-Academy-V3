Chapter 8 — The Harbour

They woke late. Not the guilty late of a missed alarm but the deliberate late of a Sunday with nothing obligatory in it, the kind that required a silent agreement made the night before without either of them saying it out loud.

The apartment in Potts Point caught the morning at an angle. The water below the hill was already active — ferries crossing, a sailing boat threading between two larger vessels — but the light through the windows was still early-quality, still arriving sideways. Sofia lay looking at the ceiling and listened to the harbour. There was a particular frequency to it. Not the water itself but the sound around the water: the low motor of the Manly ferry, the gulls, the rattle of something rigging-related carried up from Woolloomooloo, a city moving at half-speed because half of it had chosen to stay in bed.

Luca was already in the kitchen. She could smell the coffee before she heard him. They walked to a café near Circular Quay — the one with the mismatched chairs outside and the window onto the ferry terminal. The morning light here was different from Buenos Aires. She had thought about this before and kept arriving at the same word: unforgiving. Buenos Aires in the morning was gold-tinted, a city that softened its own edges at dawn. Sydney didn't do that. The light was bright without warmth in it, clarifying, the harbour a hard blue that asked nothing of you and offered nothing sentimental in return. She had come to respect it. A city that didn't flatter you was a city you could actually see.

They ordered at the counter and sat outside. The ferry terminal was fifty metres away. She watched a ferry dock, empty itself, refill, leave again. The smell of the harbour at this distance was salt and something mechanical underneath it — diesel, maybe, or the particular mineral smell of things that spent their lives partly submerged.

Luca wrapped both hands around his cup. Neither of them spoke. It was a good silence. Six weeks at Saint Peter and she was still learning the fish.

That was the honest version. The version she told herself walking to work, walking home from work, lying awake thinking about what she'd gotten wrong on the floor. Josh Niland's whole approach demanded a kind of product knowledge that most fine-dining service buried under technique — you could fold a napkin into a swan and describe a sauce using three adjectives that conveyed almost nothing, and most guests would leave satisfied. Not here. Here the menu was specific in a way that required specificity back. Which bones. Why they were there. What they did to the cooking time. What they meant for the texture at the table.

She hadn't faked it. She had asked questions that embarrassed her and looked things up and stood in the kitchen doorway watching prep until someone noticed and explained. She had done the work.

But the thing Saint Peter actually taught her wasn't fish anatomy. It took her three weeks to understand that the knowledge was only half of it.

The other half was this: guests arrived with fears they didn't name.

They said bony when they meant something else entirely. They meant I might embarrass myself in public by not knowing how to eat this properly. They meant everyone else at this table will see me struggle with it and I'll spend the next hour performing confidence I don't feel. The word bony was carrying all of that, and if you responded to the word you were responding to the wrong thing. She'd gathered the floor runners before a Tuesday service — a Wednesday, actually, she remembered now, the quieter mid-week shift — and she had said it plainly: don't respond to the word, respond to the fear under the word. Give them a different vocabulary. Describe the bone structure as architecture. As intentional. As the thing that makes the fish cook the way it cooks, that creates the texture they're going to experience, that is in fact the point and not an unfortunate consequence. Don't apologize for the bones. Explain the bones. Make them interesting before anyone has the chance to be afraid of them.

She'd told the runners: "You have to give people a different word for the thing they're afraid of. That's not spin. That's translation."

They had looked at her with the particular expression of people who had been told something they already knew and hadn't known they knew.

Standing at the harbour railing now, finishing her coffee, she thought: that's the principle under all of it. That's what the whole project is actually about.

They walked west along the promenade after breakfast, toward the Opera House. It was a route she'd done a dozen times now and it still surprised her — the way the building looked different from every angle, the way it refused to be one thing. From the ferry terminal it was massive and white and architectural. Up close the tiles were unexpected, the scale intimate in a way that photographs never communicated. She had stopped trying to figure out how it worked and started just accepting that it did.

Luca was quiet beside her. Not the silence of someone with nothing to say but the silence of someone who had been turning something over for a while and was almost ready to say it. They reached a section of railing where the promenade widened and stopped there. The ferries were doing their Sunday crossing — slower than the weekday pattern, she'd noticed, or maybe it just seemed slower because the weekend crowd moved at a different pace. The harbour was fully lit now. The light had lost whatever early softness it had possessed and become its weekday self: precise, uncompromising, good for seeing things clearly.

"I've been thinking about what William said," Luca said. "About his people staying." She knew which part. William Daniels, early in their time at Aria, had said something about retention that she and Luca had returned to several times since. That his best staff stayed not because the

money was exceptional — it wasn't, not by industry standards — but because they felt like they mattered. Not in the abstract way that motivational language produced. In the specific way: that their judgment was consulted, that their observations changed how the floor ran, that they were part of something they could see developing.

"What would that look like across the industry?" Luca asked.

She had been thinking about it too, in the way she thought about most things lately — obliquely, through the filter of whatever she was doing on the floor at Saint Peter, connecting it back to the larger shape of what they were building.

"Something that teaches people how to create that feeling," she said, "instead of just demanding it. The industry demands it all the time. Care about your guests. Be present. Make them feel seen. As if that's a thing you can command someone into doing."

"You can't teach it directly."

"No. But you can teach the conditions for it. The specific things — the hold, the sightline, the translation — those aren't techniques for their own sake. They create a situation where the feeling becomes possible. For the guest and for the server."

Luca leaned on the railing. A ferry was passing midway across the harbour, making its diagonal line from Circular Quay to somewhere she hadn't been yet.

"I keep worrying," he said, "that we're building something that falls apart when we get to Melbourne."

She waited.

"We go in different directions again," he said. "Different properties, different schedules. And the thing that makes this work is being in the same city, in the same conversation. We lose that and?" He stopped. "I don't know. It becomes theoretical. Two people working on an idea instead of one thing we're actually building together."

The water below them was green in the harbour shadow and bright blue further out where the light hit it directly. She had looked at this particular patch of water half a dozen times in the past six weeks and it had never been quite the same colour.

She turned to him. She touched his face with one hand, the way she did when she wanted him to stop the thought and hear what came next.

"Then we make the plan bigger than the contracts," she said. "ServeMaster — or whatever we end up calling it — that's the priority. Not the next job. Not the next city. This." He looked at her steadily.

"You really believe we can do this?"

She thought about the right way to say it. Not to reassure him — he wasn't asking for reassurance — but

to give him the precise thing she actually believed.

"I believe we're the only two people I've ever met who actually understand what it would take," she said. "Both of us. Together." She paused. "And I believe we're done almosting." The phrase sat between them.

She had not planned to say it that way. It had arrived whole, and she recognized it as true the way you recognized something you'd thought a hundred times in fragments finally assembled into the right shape. All of it — the cities, the conversations interrupted and resumed, the notebook with its accumulating pages, the way they kept finding each other on the other side of the industry's attempts to put distance between them — all of it had been a long approach toward something they kept getting close to and then deferring. Almost ready. Almost enough. Almost there.

Luca was quiet. Not a hesitant quiet. A quiet that was making room for what she'd said to exist in the actual air for a moment before he answered it.

"Okay," he said finally. His voice was level. "Then we keep going."

She heard both things in it.

For her, keep going meant trusting that the project would hold them when the industry tried to pull them apart, as it would, as it always did — the next opportunity, the better contract, the property that wanted only one of them. ServeMaster had to be stronger than that. Had to be the fixed point they navigated from rather than the thing they squeezed in around the edges of their actual careers. She had been building toward that belief for six weeks and it was solid now. She could put weight on it. For Luca she thought it meant something different but adjacent. He was accepting, she thought, that they were building something that might outlast any single contract — including Melbourne, including whatever came after Melbourne. He had always been good at the long view in the abstract. What she heard in his voice now was the long view made personal: not the project as career strategy but the project as the thing they were actually doing together, the real work, the one that would be there after the contracts cycled through.

Keep going was, in its way, an enormous thing to say plainly and mean it.

They found a bench in a strip of shade further along the promenade. Luca pulled out the notebook. He had started carrying it everywhere in the last month. She'd watched the habit develop — the way he reached for it in the middle of conversations, the way he read back from it to check his own thinking. The notebook had become the third presence in most of their working conversations. He read from it now. Not performing, just recapping. The 30-second hold — they had both tested it in the field and it held. The sequence: agree, reframe, disappear. The sightline principle, which had arrived from watching how the best captains in Aria moved through the room. The translation note, which was recent, which was hers from Saint Peter.

She listened. The city was doing its Sunday thing around them — a group of tourists photographing

the Opera House, a couple with a pram, a man running in the particular earnest way of someone training for something. The sky was clear and unhelpful, offering nothing but blue. "The modules," she said, thinking as she spoke. "I keep getting stuck on how to frame them." Luca looked up.

"Techniques feel wrong. Like we're teaching tricks." She turned it over. "But they're not tricks. They're?" She paused. "What if the modules aren't about techniques? What if they're about giving people a vocabulary for things they already know how to do but don't have words for yet?" He was already writing.

She watched him write and then reread what he'd written and then, without looking up, write something else underneath it.

He turned the notebook so she could see.

The module is the word, not the action.

She read it. She read it again.

"That's better than what I said."

"You said it first."

She wanted to argue the point and found she couldn't, exactly. She had said something in the neighbourhood of it. He had said the thing itself. That was the distinction she kept encountering when they worked like this — she came at things from experience, from the specific floor moment, from the particular guest and the particular fear they were carrying. He came at things from structure. Together the two produced something neither would have arrived at alone.

She hadn't had that with anyone else in this industry. She wasn't sure she'd had it anywhere. The notebook stayed open between them. The ferry terminal sounds reached them faintly. She was aware of the city going on without them, uninterested in what they were doing, indifferent in the productive way of cities.

They stayed at the railing a little longer before heading back.

She thought about the runners at Saint Peter. The one who had taken the translation idea immediately and run with it, who had come back two services later with a refinement she hadn't anticipated: not just offering a different word for the fear, but identifying when in the conversation the fear surfaced, because it wasn't always at the ordering moment. Sometimes it arrived mid-meal. Sometimes it came back after an initial success, when a guest who'd managed the first fish confidently found themselves uncertain about the second course. The runner had mapped it. Had said: the word you give them has to be available at the moment the fear returns, not just at the beginning.

She had written that down. She was going to add it to the notebook later.

She thought about the Don Julio floor, about Valentina with her notepad during family meal. About what it meant that across multiple cities and multiple properties, in good restaurants and less good ones, the same things kept mattering: the quality of translation, the dignity afforded to the guest's fear, the way a skilled server moved without announcing the movement. None of it was taught. All of it could be.

She thought about Melbourne waiting six weeks out. She thought about a name she'd heard in a café three weeks ago — Marcus Hale, mentioned in passing by someone at an adjacent table in a conversation she'd only caught a fragment of, in a context she hadn't quite understood. The name had lodged somewhere. She didn't know why. She didn't know who he was. She filed it the way she filed things that didn't yet have a place: not forgotten, just suspended.

She thought: we're done almosting. And heard it this time not as a brave thing to say — not a declaration, not an act of will — but as a plain statement of existing fact. They had been almosting for long enough that stopping was not a choice so much as a recognition. The almost period was over. They were in whatever came next.

Luca closed the notebook. He didn't say anything. She didn't say anything either. They turned and walked back the way they'd come, past the bench, past the place at the railing where she'd touched his face, past the ferry terminal with its Sunday afternoon rhythm. The city was the same city it had been that morning. The light had moved and was now doing something different with the water — longer shadows, the angle shifted. The smell of the harbour was still salt and diesel and the particular weight of something old and constantly in use.

They walked in the particular silence of people who have arrived at something and are letting it settle before they have to name it again.

The project was real. The project was what came next.

That was enough for a Sunday.
