Signora Rossi's hug smelled like bread flour and lavender soap, and it lasted long enough that Sofia's ribs ached in a way that felt like being forgiven for leaving.

"You grew here, ragazza." The old woman's voice was thick against Sofia's shoulder. "Florence marks you. Take it with you."

Sofia nodded, throat tight, and kissed both cheeks, and picked up the rolling suitcase Signora Rossi had insisted she borrow because her backpack had a broken zipper and because, as Signora Rossi put it, "a woman with only a backpack looks like she is running from something, a woman with a suitcase looks like she is going toward it." The logic was Italian and unassailable.

The walk to Gucci Osteria felt like a procession through a city that was already becoming memory. The Arno glittered under the Ponte Vecchio. The jewelry shops winked gold. At the edge of the Mercato Centrale, the dawn bustle was already in full throat — vendors shouting over prosciutto slicers, blood oranges stacked in pyramids that looked too perfect to be real and too real to be art. She wanted a last tripe sandwich and there wasn't time, which felt like a metaphor she didn't want to examine too closely.

Elena found her in the pass-through, and the hug was fierce and fast, the way Elena did everything. "You'll conquer that chaos. Remember —" and here she tapped Sofia's hip, then pointed up, which was not a sentence but was understood: *tray here, eyes there, always*. Marco clapped her shoulder and pressed a paper-wrapped package into her hands — leftover tortellini in brodo, still warm — and said, "Doors open. Go chase the fire," and Sofia blinked hard and blamed the kitchen steam.

She wasn't the wide-eyed girl who'd stepped off the train at Santa Maria Novella six months ago. She knew that the way you know you've grown out of a coat — not because anyone tells you, but because it simply doesn't fit anymore. The girl who'd dropped a tray her first week and stood in the walk-in with shaking hands had become someone who read a room from the door, who felt a service falling behind in her chest before the kitchen pinged, who could pour with her eyes on the table three seats over.

But the hunger had deepened, not quieted. And it was pulling her south.

---

Barcelona's Sants station hit like a wall of sound and salt. No elegant piazzas. No Arno slipping quietly underneath. Just bodies surging in every direction, announcements barking in Catalan over the screech of arriving trains, the fizz of cans opening from vendors' coolers mixing with cigarette smoke and the particular electric smell of metro rails.

She loved it immediately.

The metro rattled underground through graffiti-streaked tunnels and emerged into Mediterranean light — palm trees against brutalist concrete, the distant crash of waves under the Barceloneta boardwalk, the sky a shade of blue that Florence never quite managed, as though Barcelona's sky had less patience and more confidence.

Her flat was in El Born's labyrinth, third floor up a stairwell that echoed with neighbours' arguments and the sizzle of paella pans. Maria — her flatmate, a wiry Catalan bartender with tattoo sleeves and a grin that suggested trouble was an active hobby — handed over the key.

"Bienvenida. Rules: no fish in the fridge, and the party starts at midnight."

The room was tiny. Bed wedged against a window overlooking laundry lines strung like flags between buildings. She unpacked in twelve minutes, laid out her uniform, and sat on the bed listening to Barcelona breathe through the open window. Guitars from somewhere. A moped. A child's shriek of laughter, then a mother's voice, warm and sharp. The smell of grilled sardines drifting up from a restaurant below that she would learn, over the coming weeks, was run by a woman named Pilar who sang flamenco while cooking and never took reservations because she believed the right people would simply arrive.

Tomorrow: Quimet i Quimet. Standing-room tapas. Chaos as art form. Her pulse quickened.

---

Quimet i Quimet crouched on Carrer del Poeta Cabanyes in Poble Sec like a secret everyone knew but nobody had made boring yet. Narrow façade between a hardware store and a pharmacy. Barely twenty square metres inside. High wooden shelves groaned with tinned conservas — sepia in ink, razor clams in brine, sardines packed so perfectly they looked like small silver soldiers in formation. No tables. No chairs worth mentioning. A marble bar worn smooth from generations of elbows.

Quim, third generation, had a moustache that looked like it predated the restaurant and a way of looking at people that suggested he'd already decided whether he liked them and the introduction was a formality.

He looked at Sofia. Nodded once.

"Shadow Clara. Watch her hands."

Clara was mid-twenties, ponytail pulled tight, and moved behind the bar the way a boxer moves in a ring — economical, aware, never looking where she was going because she already knew. She thrust a towel at Sofia. "We turn twenty an hour at peak. Read their eyes, not their mouths."

The rush hit at eleven like a wave hitting a seawall.

Locals flooded in post-market, elbows claiming inches of bar space, voices layering over each other in rapid Catalan and Spanish and something that might have been French from the couple wedged near the door. Sofia gripped a tray — smaller than Gucci's, loaded with montaditos: anchovies on toast crowned with pickled guindilla, bread crunching under pressure. Glasses of moscatel de Sitges, pale gold, fizzing.

The crush tested everything Florence had built. A burly man in paint-splattered overalls waved two fingers — two bombas. She pivoted to the fryer, oil popping, Clara dropping fresh batches with the rhythm of someone who could do this in her sleep and probably had. Back through the press, shoulders grazing strangers, tray held at hip height. A woman in a sundress bumped her reaching elbow and the tray wobbled — a bomba sliding toward the edge.

Instinct. Elbow snapped in, weight shifted over her left foot, tray levelled in a motion so clean she barely registered making it. The bomba stopped. The woman murmured *perdón*. Sofia was already placing plates — first the worker's mate, then his, napkins tucked, moving on. The elderly couple eyeing the vitello tonnato. The three suits barking for txakoli.

This was not Alo's quiet choreography. This was not Gucci Osteria's elegant theatre. This was standing-room instinct at full volume, and it felt like coming home to a house she'd never been in but whose floor plan she already knew.

---

Peak at one o'clock. The bar was a solid mass of bodies, air thick with fried potato and tinned fish brine and the kind of laughter that happens when people are eating well and standing close together and have decided not to care about personal space. Sofia moved through it on something beyond thought — reading the lean-in hunger of the market vendor who wanted razor clams, the subtle head-tilt of the tourist couple ready for the house vermut, the flicker in a father's eyes as his kid tugged his sleeve.

She poured standing, clockwise from the hungriest first, twist at the finish to catch the drop, glass slid across wet marble without a spill. Every montadito topped right — foie gras with fig jam for the man in linen, plain pan tumaca for the workman, the distinction made without looking at her hands because her hands already knew.

At two, the near-disaster. Five deep at the bar, kitchen slamming out cod fritters, her tray overloaded — three raciones, four vermuts, two moscatels. The door swung wide and a fresh wave pushed through and a shoulder caught the tray hard enough to send everything sliding.

Her heart slammed once. One beat.

Hip locked. Thumb jammed under the tray edge — the Florence grip, Elena's gift, muscle memory deeper than thought. Free hand caught the top glass a centimetre from the edge. Everything stopped moving. Everything was still on the tray.

She exhaled. Continued. Distributed clockwise, starting with the eyes that had been waiting longest.

Quim watched from the pass. Grunted. "Buen salvamento."

By five, her shirt was soaked through and her legs burned and she was grinning so hard her face ached.

---

Barcelona's evenings were not Florence's quiet riverside walks. Barcelona's evenings were *loud* — bars spilling onto pavements, glasses of tinto de verano sweating in the heat, Gothic facades catching the last orange light and throwing it back in shadows that looked like they'd been there since the Middle Ages because they had.

She wandered El Born after shifts. Gas lamps flickered on as the streets narrowed. Street musicians strummed flamenco riffs with a speed and ferocity that made her fingers ache in sympathy. The smell of grilled sardines threaded through everything, mixing with jasmine from someone's balcony and cigarette smoke and the warm bread smell that seemed to come from nowhere specific and everywhere at once.

One night, rain caught her in the Barri Gòtic. Not gentle Florence rain — a proper Mediterranean assault, warm and sudden and committed. She dashed under a church portico and leaned against cold stone as water sheeted off the gargoyles above and turned the narrow streets to mirrors. Lightning cracked. The gargoyles leered. She stood alone in the downpour, breathing, and felt the city's pulse in the wet stone under her hands.

Florence's quiet Arno felt like a letter from someone she used to be. This wilder rhythm matched her blood now.

The rain eased. She walked on, wet and not caring, Gothic spires piercing the clearing clouds.

---

Midweek she enrolled at Le Cordon Bleu Barcelona — the campus sleek and modern in the Eixample, glass walls overlooking Gaudí's modernist blocks. The advisor scanned her Gucci Osteria reference and the deferred acceptance from Paris and said, with the particular satisfaction of someone recognizing a story they'd seen before: "Earned now."

Classes started Mondays. Wine foundations. Service precision. The textbooks under her arm felt different than they would have six months ago — not aspirational, but confirmational. Not *learn this so you can do the thing*. More *here is the language for what your body already knows*.

---

Last shift at Quimet. Post-rush, the bar emptying to its familiar sticky quiet, Quim pulling her aside while Clara wiped the marble with the same cloth she'd been using for what appeared to be the entire twenty-first century.

"Heard Disfrutar's looking." He said it casually, the way people deliver information they know is not casual. "Three stars. Villarroel 163. Oriol Castro's crew. Avant-garde. You got the speed."

Disfrutar. She'd read about it on the train from Florence — molecular, creative, the kind of restaurant where a course might arrive as a cloud or a memory or an olive that exploded in your mouth into something that was technically a gazpacho but emotionally was the entire coast of Andalucía.

Her pulse jumped. Quim saw it and allowed himself the smallest smile.

"Apply," he said. "Tell them Quim sent you. It won't help. But tell them anyway."

She biked home through streets that smelled like the sea and the last garlic of someone's midnight dinner, and sat on her tiny bed by the window, and thought about the girl in the parking lot in Brampton who'd been fired after three weeks and had believed, briefly, that she was unsuited for this.

That girl lived in a country Sofia could see from here but no longer inhabited.

She opened her notebook — the one she kept for observations, not recipes — and wrote:

*Quimet. Standing-room tray save at 14:00. Shoulder clip. Florence grip held. Quim grunted — this is his highest form of praise. Clara called me family. Disfrutar next. Three stars. The hunger is getting louder.*

She closed the notebook. Outside, El Born hummed. Laundry flapped on the lines. Pilar was singing downstairs.
