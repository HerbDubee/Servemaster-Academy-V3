Chapter 7 — Sydney

The email arrived on a Tuesday. It was three sentences long. The visa extension request had been reviewed, the outcome was unfortunately negative, and they were welcome to reapply from outside Argentina within thirty days of the notification date. Sofia read it at the kitchen table in the Palermo apartment and then set her phone face-down and finished her coffee.

Luca was still asleep. She didn't wake him.

Later that morning, sitting across from him with the second pot between them, she said: "We have thirty days."

He looked at her. "Then we use them."

And they did. They worked their last weeks in Buenos Aires the way they had learned to work: fully present, nothing left in reserve. Sofia picked up extra lunch shifts at Tegui. Luca ran two private dining events at a friend-of-a-friend's estancia outside the city and took careful notes on everything that went wrong. They cooked for each other on the nights they were both off. They walked along the river at times they hadn't managed before because there had always been something else to do.

Thirty days is a long time if you use it well. They did.

The flight to Sydney was fourteen hours. They sat together in economy, shoulders touching, and somewhere over the Pacific, Luca fell asleep mid-sentence and Sofia watched the flight path move slowly across the screen and wrote three pages in the notebook she had started keeping in Buenos Aires.

The Potts Point apartment was on the fourth floor of a building that was older than it looked from the outside. The view from the window faced east: a slice of the harbour, Darlinghurst rooftops in the middle distance, and when the light was right in the early morning, the city's skyline visible in hard, clean lines against a sky that was rarely as blue as you expected it to be from photographs. Sydney was cooler than Buenos Aires. The light was different — sharper, less forgiving, with a quality that made everything look slightly more exposed. The apartment was smaller than the one they had left, and more expensive, and Luca spent the first three days walking the city in expanding circles trying to understand it.

Buenos Aires had wanted you to love it. It asked for that directly. Sydney didn't ask for anything. It simply existed with complete confidence, and if you found your way into it — through the harbour walk, or the morning light off the water at Manly, or the strange intimacy of its inner suburbs — that discovery felt like something the city had allowed rather than offered. It earned attention differently. Luca wrote that in the notebook and then looked at it for a long time, not sure yet what it meant for the work.

He started at Quay at the beginning of March. Sofia walked up the hill to Saint Peter on her first Monday and came home that night smelling of the sea.

Quay sat at East Circular Quay, the dining room angled toward the harbour with the Opera House visible through the glass like something from a painting that other paintings had spent decades quoting. Peter Gilmore's kitchen sent out food that required a particular kind of attention from the floor — not the theatrical attention of somewhere trying to be important, but the precise, nearly invisible attention of somewhere that already knew what it was. The guests had researched the menu before they arrived. They knew the ingredients. Some of them had read the sourcing notes on the website. They came in prepared, which meant that any failure in service registered with unusual clarity.

The room rewarded stillness.

That was what Luca saw in his first two weeks of service, watching how the established floor staff moved. Singapore had taught him to read the room at speed — to absorb the emotional temperature of a space quickly and adjust. But at Quay the game was different. Speed here read as anxiety. What the room wanted was composed deceleration: the visible unhurriedness that made guests feel they were the only table in the room even when the kitchen had called a simultaneous hold on three and the expediter was running two minutes behind and the sommelier was deep in a conversation at seven that was going to pull him from five for at least another four minutes. The room absorbed all of that and reflected none of it. The floor's job was to be the room.

The staff were good. Most of them were very good. Jamie was not bad.

Jamie was twenty-two, from Wollongong, had been working hospitality since he was sixteen and had the instincts that came from genuine experience — he could read a drink order in a glance, he knew when a table wanted to linger and when it wanted to move, and his menu knowledge was solid because he'd actually eaten the tasting menu twice on his own money, which told you something about him. But he moved like he was proving something. There was a quality of visible effort in the way he crossed the room, a performance of competence that was almost the same thing as competence but not quite. Luca had seen it before. He recognised it because he had had some version of it himself, years ago, in the first restaurant where he had started to be taken seriously. The effort showed. And in a room like Quay, effort that showed was a kind of noise.

He watched Jamie through a full service on the second Thursday in March.

The early covers went fine. Table four: a couple celebrating something, rings new enough to catch the light. Jamie handled them with appropriate warmth, letting the occasion breathe, not overcrowding the moments. Table two: a corporate dinner, four people, subtle competition for conversational territory. Jamie read the senior person correctly and directed most of his information there without ignoring the others. Good instincts. Working.

Table nine came in at seven forty-five. A woman in her mid-fifties and a man perhaps a few years older, the kind of couple who had learned to be comfortable in restaurants but still chose their words carefully in them. They ordered without fuss. They were mid-conversation — something personal, something the woman had been working toward saying for some time, you could see it in the way she held the pause before she spoke — when Jamie came to the table for the second course.

He was carrying four plates. The configuration was technically correct: two arms, a tray for the third and fourth, the plates stacked with appropriate gaps. But when he came to the near side of Table 9 to set the first plate down, he came in from the right. The guest's right. His arm crossed the space in front of the woman at the precise moment she was mid-sentence, and her gaze — tracking her husband's face — broke around Jamie's forearm and lost its thread.

The plates landed without incident. The food was flawless. The woman smiled politely when Jamie named the dish and then turned back to her husband.

But the sentence was gone. Whatever she had been moving toward saying, she had arrived at it differently now, on the other side of an interruption, and the thing you almost said and the thing you say after a small derailment are not always the same thing.

The woman didn't say anything. She simply reset with the particular patience of someone making an assessment — not of the food, not of the evening — of the room itself, of whether it was worth the effort.

After service, Luca found Jamie in the back corridor. He was checking the linen count, moving through it quickly, the post-shift gear-down already visible in his shoulders.

"Do you know what happened on Table 9?" Luca said.

Jamie turned. He was already running the service backward in his head; you could see it. "The plates?"

"It's not the plates," Luca said. "The plates landed fine. The sequence was right. Your carry was clean."

Jamie waited.

"It's your elbow," Luca said. "Your elbow came across her line of sight while she was mid-sentence with her husband. She was telling him something. Something she'd been building toward. Your elbow interrupted it."

Jamie's expression changed. Not defensiveness — something closer to genuine recognition, the particular look of someone who has just seen a thing they cannot unsee.

"She didn't say anything," he said.

"No," Luca said. "She wouldn't."

He thought about Florence for a half-second — not consciously, not as a memory he was reaching for, but the way a reflex arrives before thought: Sofia's thumb finding the edge of the tray, her elbow pulling back without breaking stride, the way she had shown him later at a bar table how the geometry of a tray carry was actually a geometry of sightlines. He had written it down that night in a notebook that no longer existed. He had been writing versions of it ever since.

"You can't unsee it once you see it," he said. It was almost exactly what Sofia had told him then. It was still true. "The tray isn't a logistics problem. It's not about weight distribution or carry angle. It's a sightline problem. Your job is to be invisible. And invisible means knowing where everyone's eyes are before you move. Every time. Not as a rule you're following. As something you actually see."

Jamie was quiet. He was doing the thing Luca recognised — running the service backward now with different eyes, replotting every approach he'd made.

"Come here," Luca said.

He showed him the corridor, pointing out an imaginary table. Marked a spot on the floor with his foot.

"Guest's left. Always left when there's a conversation happening and the guest's eyeline is toward the centre of the table. Tray stays below shoulder height — below the eye line of the people seated, not standing. You never cross an occupied sightline on a first pass. Go around. Even if it costs you three steps. Three steps is nothing. An interrupted sentence is something."

He walked the approach twice. Jamie watched.

"Now you."

Jamie walked it. First time a little stiff, the performance of correctness. Second time better, the mechanics quieter. Third time, almost nothing — the movement itself, the approach angle, the arc around the imaginary sightline, all of it simply present without comment.

"That's it," Luca said.

Jamie nodded. He didn't say anything else. He went back to the linen count.

On the following service, Table 9 was seated again — different guests, different conversation — and Luca watched Jamie from across the room. The approach: guest's left, tray low, arc around the sightline as smooth as if it had always been there. One of the guests at the table kept talking through the plate set. Didn't pause. Didn't look up. The food simply arrived, and Jamie retreated, and the conversation continued uninterrupted.

At the end of service, the Table 9 server found a note folded into the bill wallet.

Impeccable.

The note made it to the pass. It made it to the manager. The manager left it on the corkboard in the back corridor and wrote nothing beside it, which was the kind of commentary Luca had learned to respect.

He photographed it with his phone and sent it to Sofia.

She took seven minutes to reply. She was probably still on the floor.

That's Module 4.

He read it twice standing in the corridor. Then he went and sat in the narrow locker area, the restaurant noise fading through the door, and opened the notebook. He wrote:

The sightline principle: your job is to be invisible, which means knowing where everyone is looking before you move.

And then below it, because it needed something more precise:

Not logistics. Geometry of care.

He looked at it for a moment. Then he closed the notebook and went back out to help with the reset.

Sofia's first week at Saint Peter, she learned fish.

This was not a small thing. Josh Niland's whole-fish restaurant on Oxford Street had built its reputation on the idea that fish deserved the same nose-to-tail philosophy that had already transformed how good restaurants thought about meat, and the floor staff were expected to carry that philosophy through every conversation they had with a guest. Dry-aged versus fresh catch. The difference in texture, fat distribution, flavour concentration — and why those differences mattered for a specific dish, on a specific night, with a specific preparation. Guests who came to Saint Peter had often already read about dry-aging. They came in curious. Sometimes they came in sceptical. The room couldn't afford floor staff who deflected.

Sofia spent her first four days reading everything she could find about fish butchery. She stood in the kitchen during prep twice and watched the cooks break down the whole fish, asking questions until the answers stopped feeling like vocabulary and started feeling like understanding. By the end of the week she could talk about a dry-aged hapuka — its denser texture, the way the moisture loss concentrated the flavour, the correct way to describe it to someone who had never encountered the category — in a way that was accurate and also genuinely enthusiastic, because she had found the thing genuinely interesting.

Which was the only way it worked. You couldn't perform that kind of knowledge. Guests felt the performance. You had to actually know.

In her second week, she started working with the runners.

The runners were good at describing the fish — they had the words, they had the provenance — but they had a habit that she noticed in the third service and couldn't stop noticing afterward. When guests asked about bones, the runners said bony. As in: this one's a bit bony, just watch for the pin bones. The word was accurate. The word was also, she realised, exactly the wrong word. People heard bony and they heard a warning, an apology in advance, a small concession that the fish had a flaw that required management. Which was not the truth of it at all. The bones in a whole fish were architecture. They were the structure the flesh organised itself around. They were part of why the flesh tasted like it did.

On a Thursday, she pulled the runners together for five minutes before service.

"When someone asks about bones," she said, "don't say bony. Tell them about the structure. Tell them how the flesh sits closer to the bone than a fillet and that's where the most flavour lives. Give them a different word for the thing they're afraid of. That's not spin. That's translation."

The runners looked at her. One of them — Priya, who had been there eight months and had a natural gentleness with guests — wrote it down.

That night, walking home up the hill to Potts Point, Sofia thought: that's the most ServeMaster thing I've said all month. She had not used the word ServeMaster out loud to anyone here. It existed in the notebooks, in the late-night conversations over the Palermo kitchen table that felt more serious as the months passed, in the slow accumulation of named principles that were starting to feel like something that had a shape. Not a product yet. Not a curriculum. But a shape.

She wrote it down when she got home: Translation, not spin.

On Friday night she told Luca about the runners, about bony versus architecture, about finding the right word for the thing someone is afraid of.

He got up and went for the notebook. He wrote Translation, not spin under the sightline entry.

He sat back and looked at the page.

"We're getting better at naming things," he said.

"Or the things are getting clearer," Sofia said.

He thought about that. "Maybe both."

The Melbourne posting appeared on a Tuesday in late April. Sofia found it on her laptop during a slow afternoon between services, forwarded it to herself, and then closed the tab and didn't open it again for two days. When she did, she sat with it for an hour before she said anything to Luca.

She could have sent him a screenshot. She could have mentioned it over dinner. Instead she waited until he was home from a late service at Quay — it was past eleven, the city quiet outside the apartment windows, the harbour dark except for the ferry lights — and when he came through the door and kicked off his shoes, she was on the couch with the laptop open.

He saw the screen.

"Show me."

She handed him the laptop.

Six-month contract. A new restaurant in South Yarra, strong team, someone whose work they both respected in the kitchen. The money was good. The timeline: position available in eight weeks.

He read it twice. Set the laptop down on the coffee table.

"What do you want?" he said.

Sofia had been thinking about this for two days and she still didn't have an answer that felt complete, so she gave him the one she had: "I want to stop choosing between you and the work. I keep taking the right jobs in the right cities and they're not in the same place and I'm tired of the math. I want to build something that makes that math different."

He looked at her.

"Then let's start treating it like it's real," he said. "Not a someday dream. Something with a timeline."

She pulled her legs up on the couch. "I might still take Melbourne."

"I know."

"That's eight weeks from now. That's not nothing."

"Eight weeks is a long time if you use it."

She looked at him. He was looking at the notebook on the side table, or maybe at the window, or maybe at something she couldn't quite locate. He was thinking. She'd learned to recognise that particular quality of his attention — when he was working through something in real time and didn't yet have the shape of it.

"Okay," she said. "Then let's use it."

They stayed up past three. The notebook filled — not with principles this time, not with named modules or technique breakdowns, but with larger things: what a curriculum might actually look like, built from everything they'd seen and learned and named over two years. What the floor taught that couldn't be transmitted in a classroom. How you built for both without losing either. The specific things they kept telling people in back corridors after service that the people needed to hear — and the question of whether those things could be taught before the corridor, before the mistake, before the moment.

At some point they ran out of clean structure and started writing in circles, and at some later point — Sofia genuinely could not remember who said it first, whether it came out of a sentence or between sentences, whether it was proposed or simply arrived — one of them said ServeMaster.

Not as a decision. Not as a formal declaration. As a word that fit the shape of the thing they'd been describing for the last four hours.

The room went quiet for a second.

Then Luca said: "It'll probably change."

"Probably," Sofia said.

But neither of them wrote over it. The word sat at the top of the last page, circled twice by whichever of them had been holding the pen at that moment, and they both looked at it for a moment before Sofia closed the notebook.

The harbour was still dark outside. In a few hours the light would start coming in from the east, and the city would resolve back into its sharp lines, and they would need to sleep before the next service.

Melbourne was eight weeks away. It wasn't close, but it was close enough to move toward. Both of them, for the first time, moving toward it on purpose.
