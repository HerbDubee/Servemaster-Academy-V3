Chapter 6 — The Visa Question

The heat came early in February. By eight in the morning it was already sitting on the city like something patient and deliberate, and Sofia had dragged her chair to the far corner of the balcony where the building's shadow held for another half hour. She had her coffee. She had the sound of a delivery motorbike laboring up Gorriti, its engine cycling and catching, cycling and catching. From somewhere in the building above her, a neighbor's radio played cumbia at a volume that suggested the neighbor had not yet gone to sleep.

She was not unhappy. This was the specific texture of a Buenos Aires Tuesday in summer — heat, noise, the smell of warm concrete — and she had learned to read it as its own kind of weather rather than a complaint about something.

Her phone lit up on the arm of the chair.

She picked it up without urgency. Set down the coffee.

The email was from the restaurant's legal contact, a woman named Valentina who communicated exclusively in bullet points and whose neutrality of tone Sofia had, over eight months, come to understand as its own form of careful kindness. The subject line was: RE: Work Visa Extension — Update.

Sofia read it once. Then again. Then a third time, which changed nothing.

The Argentine work visa expired in six weeks. The restaurant had submitted the extension paperwork in January, as required. The processing timeline was unpredictable. There was no guarantee the extension would be approved before the expiry date. If it was not approved in time, she would need to exit the country and re-enter under a different visa category. Valentina had attached a list of options. All of them required leaving.

The motorbike reached the top of the hill and disappeared. The radio upstairs switched to something slower.

Sofia looked out over the rooflines of Palermo toward the flat horizon where the city simply kept going until it decided to stop. She felt the email settle into her chest — not panic, something more organized than that. The industry had handed her versions of this problem before: the contract that ended a month early, the permit that required a different classification than the one she held, the city that wanted her until it didn't. She knew how to categorize a problem like this. She knew the steps.

What was new — what she was not yet sure she knew how to categorize — was that this particular problem had a second dimension the others hadn't. The others had only required her to plan for herself.

She didn't go inside to wake Luca.

She finished her coffee. She watched the shadow retreat from the corner of the balcony. She picked up the phone once more and looked at the email a fourth time, not because she expected the words to change but because she had learned, professionally and otherwise, that re-reading something you don't like is a form of honesty.

Then she got up, went inside, and began getting ready for her lunch shift.

Don Julio on a Tuesday in February ran two different restaurants at once: the one the guests understood themselves to be in, and the one that actually existed behind the pass. Sofia had worked there long enough to hold both in mind simultaneously, which was the only way to work there at all.

The table of twelve arrived at one-thirty, thirty minutes past their reservation. They arrived the way large groups always arrived late — without apology, with the particular energy of people who had already decided the lunch would be excellent and had assigned responsibility for that excellence to someone else. Sofia counted them in as they moved toward the large table in the back corner. She was already rearranging the sequence in her head.

The kitchen had been behind since noon. A supplier had delivered the wrong cut, and the preparation timeline for the tira de asado had shifted by forty minutes. The sous chef had not communicated this to the floor. Sofia had found out by asking, which was how she always found out the things that mattered.

She had the junior runner, Mateo, on the section — twenty-two years old, capable, nervous in a way that made him fast. She stationed him at the beverage pass and told him she would handle the approach on the twelve herself.

"Buenas tardes." She let them settle for a moment first, let the last person find her chair and the conversation redistribute itself the way it always did when a group finally stopped moving. Then: "Welcome. I'm glad you're here. We have a full menu available for you this afternoon."

She did not mention the delay. She did not mention the kitchen. She took the aperitivo order from the host, confirmed two dietary restrictions without making a production of them, and excused herself at the exact moment the table's attention turned back inward.

The visa email sat in her chest.

She carried it through the first course the way she'd learned to carry everything she wasn't supposed to show: tucked behind the sternum, weight distributed evenly, nothing leaking into her face or her pace or the angle at which she set a plate down. She had not intended to get good at this. It was a skill the industry had installed without asking.

The problem arrived at the forty-minute mark, which was when the kitchen was still behind and the table of twelve had finished their empanadas and were beginning to notice the silence. She saw it in the posture of the man at the head of the table — mid-fifties, traveled enough to know what he expected, the particular way he set down his wine glass with slightly more weight than necessary.

She reached the table before he could lift his hand.

"I wanted to give you a moment before the next course," she said. "The kitchen is finishing the preparation now. You'll have time to breathe between plates."

She said it to the table, not to him specifically, which was deliberate.

He said: "We did arrive a bit later than planned." He paused. "I hope that hasn't created complications."

The subtext was plain. Sofia heard it. The table heard it.

"The kitchen paces to the table," she said. "You're right on time."

Brief. Accurate. She did not apologize for the kitchen. She did not correct his implication directly. She simply described a reality in which his lateness was already accounted for, which made it, retroactively, not a problem at all. The sentence had nowhere to push against.

The man nodded. The woman beside him asked a question about the Malbec, and the moment dissolved.

Mateo appeared at her elbow as she moved back toward the pass. "They're plating now," he said. "Four minutes."

"Good. Stay on water."

She carried the main course out in two runs. The table quieted in the way tables always quieted when the food was right — the specific, grateful silence of people who had momentarily stopped performing and started eating. Sofia read the shift and eased back, giving them room. She checked the other two sections. She ran a coffee order to the two-top by the window. She kept moving.

The visa email did not go away. It sat where she'd put it, steady and factual, and she carried it through the dessert course and the final coffee service and the moment when the host of the twelve pressed her hand and said the lunch had been exceptional, and she smiled and meant the thank you, because she had worked for it.

After service, she went to the staff room. There was a sink in the corner and she ran cold water over her wrists — an old habit, something about the temperature — and looked at the wall and understood something that had been assembling itself for the past two hours.

The hardest thing about this job was not the guests.

It was not the kitchen, or the timing, or the table of twelve that had arrived late and expected the math to still come out even. Those were problems. She knew how to solve problems.

The hardest thing was learning to protect the room from whatever was happening inside you.

She had done it today with the visa email. She had done it before with other things — the call from home that came before a Saturday dinner service, the day she'd found out a contract wasn't being renewed midway through a wedding reception, the headache that had lived behind her left eye for three days during a particularly bad week in Singapore. The room had never felt any of it. That was the job. The room couldn't feel it.

But here was the thing she hadn't let herself name before: that protection was active. It wasn't endurance. It wasn't performing normalcy while suffering privately. It was a skill — something she had built and refined and could now deploy with precision, the same way she could time a course or read a table's energy from across the room.

A good server was always in two places at once. Present in the room. Carrying something outside it that the room must never feel.

She stood at the sink a moment longer. She would add that to the notebook later. Not now — it was too new and she wanted to make sure she understood exactly what she meant before she wrote it down, because her notebook had no patience for imprecision.

She changed out of her work shirt. She folded it carefully, the way she always did, for reasons she'd never examined. Then she walked home through the February heat, which had not diminished, which would not diminish until well after dark.

Luca was in the apartment when she got home, reading at the table with his shoes off. He looked up when she came in, and she watched the shift happen in his face — the particular calibration he did when he was reading her — and before she said anything he'd already set the book down.

She didn't explain. She took her phone from her pocket and handed it to him, open to the email.

He read it. She got a glass of water and stood by the kitchen counter and watched the courtyard through the window. A cat was moving along the top of the far wall with the particular composure of an animal that had never questioned its right to be where it was.

Luca set the phone down on the table. He didn't say anything immediately. She appreciated that. The industry had trained her to distrust people who filled silences.

"How long do we have?" he said finally.

"Six weeks. Maybe less."

She came to the table and sat down. The apartment felt different in February than it had in January — not smaller, but more specific, the way a place became more itself after you'd been in it long enough. She had not quite admitted to herself that she had started to think of it as a place she lived rather than a place she was staying.

Luca was quiet for another moment. She didn't interpret the silence. She let it do what it was doing.

"So let's stop letting it decide for us," he said.

She looked at him.

He meant it. She could see that he meant it, which was its own separate thing from understanding exactly what he meant. She waited.

"What does that mean, practically?" she said.

"It means if the extension doesn't come through, we figure out the next city together. Not separately."

The word together sat in the room. She was not a person who used that word carelessly, and she had noticed — had been noticing, without recording the observation anywhere — that Luca wasn't either. He'd just used it as the axis of a practical proposal. That was different from sentiment. That was a different category of thing entirely.

She looked at him for a long moment. She was assessing, not softening. The industry had given her fluency in the difference between people who said things like this and people who would honor them six weeks later when the complication arrived in its full shape. She had been wrong before. She had the record of being wrong in cities she no longer lived in.

He didn't look away. He was letting her look, which was exactly right.

She leaned her head against his shoulder.

They stayed like that for a while. Outside, the courtyard was still. The cat had moved on.

"I don't know how to do this any other way," she said. She meant the industry — the accumulated logic of contracts and visas and cities that didn't overlap neatly, the operational grammar she had spent eight years learning to navigate alone. But she also meant something she didn't quite have language for yet, something more private: she had always been the person who planned for the ending. She knew the shape of leaving. She didn't know the shape of staying. Staying had never been a category she'd allowed herself to practice.

"Neither do I," Luca said. "But I think we're about to find out."

She didn't answer that. She didn't need to. The city outside the window made its indifferent nighttime sounds — traffic on the boulevard, someone's terrace door, the specific urban quiet that was really just noise below a certain threshold — and she felt the visa email still there in her chest, but repositioned somehow. Not heavier. Just differently weighted, like something you've been carrying in one hand that you've shifted to the other.

They didn't solve the visa problem that night.

What they did, over the next two hours, was change the frame around it. They talked about the future in a way they hadn't quite talked about it before — not as a series of contracts that would eventually intersect or not, not as an industry timeline that would eventually produce a window, but as something that might have a design. An intent. Something authored rather than accepted.

Sofia opened her laptop. She had been tracking the market in Australia for longer than she'd admitted to herself — not obsessively, just the way she tracked all markets, with the professional habit of knowing what was available before she needed it. Melbourne had two openings that were still live. Sydney had one she hadn't fully investigated.

"There's a place in Melbourne that's been asking for a floor manager since October," she said.

"I've had a conversation with someone at Attica," Luca said. He said it simply, without positioning, which meant he'd been tracking it too.

She looked at him.

"October," she said.

"Around then."

Neither of them said anything for a moment. They were both doing the same calculation, which was not really a calculation — it was more the recognition that they had independently been pointing themselves toward the same coordinates without discussing it. The industry made you secretive about your intentions. It was a survival behavior. It was also, she was starting to understand, not automatically a good habit.

She went back to the laptop. She looked at the Melbourne listing longer than she needed to. Then she reached for the notebook on the nightstand — it had migrated there at some point in the last two months, she'd noticed this without remarking on it — and she opened it to a clean page.

She wrote one line. Not about service. Not about a guest, or a technique, or a kitchen, or anything the notebook usually held.

I am more afraid of designing my own life than I ever was of the industry designing it for me.

She looked at the line. She considered it the way she considered everything she wrote — not for whether she liked it, but for whether it was true.

It was true.

She closed the notebook. Luca was already asleep beside her, his breathing slow and even in the warm room. The city outside was indifferent in the particular way of cities in summer — it had its own business, its own scale, and she had never been under any illusion that it required her specifically.

She didn't delete the line.

She put the notebook back on the nightstand and turned off the lamp and lay in the dark of the Buenos Aires February, carrying what she was carrying, already beginning to learn its shape.
