The wine exam room at Le Cordon Bleu Barcelona smelled like oak polish and quiet terror.

Twenty students at twenty stations, each one a mock dining setup — crisp white linen, crystal stemware, bottles and decanters arranged with the formality of a chess opening. Instructors prowled the perimeter in dark suits, clipboards in hand, their expressions suggesting they had been born disappointed and had only grown more refined in their disappointment over time.

Sofia stood at station five, hands still at her sides, and breathed.

She'd been building toward this for months. The flashcards spread across her bed in El Born at midnight, Maria stepping over them on her way to the bathroom and saying *you're insane* with genuine admiration. The Saturday afternoons spent in the campus library, tasting notes accumulating in her notebook like pressed flowers — each one a moment preserved, a flavour pinned to a memory. The hours at Disfrutar, watching their sommelier present bottles with the quiet authority of someone offering guests a key to a room they didn't know existed.

"Estación cinco. Comience."

Her station lit up. Four simulated guests — two instructors playing a celebrating couple, two playing the kind of discerning diners who would notice if her pour was three millilitres off. The menu called for a Rioja Reserva with the jamón ibérico starter, transitioning to a Priorat for the grilled octopus.

She picked up the bottle. Held it so the label faced the host, catching the light the way Carla had shown her, the way she'd practised until her wrist found the angle by feel rather than thought.

"For your jamón — the 2018 La Rioja Alta Gran Reserva." Her voice was steady. Not performing steadiness. Actually steady. "Black cherry, vanilla oak, tannins firm enough to cut the fat. I think you'll enjoy it."

She paused. Scanned their faces. The small inclination of heads — not dramatic, just present.

Then the pour. Clockwise from the right. Hostess first. The wine fell into the tulip glass in a single unbroken stream, filling to the widest point where the bouquet would gather. No glug. No splash. At the finish, the twist — wrist turning to sever the stream clean — and the linen touch across the lip, one motion, no smear. Next glass. Next. Next. Each pour identical, as though the wine itself had agreed to cooperate.

She stepped back. Hands at her waist. Watched them swirl and sip and nod.

The Priorat followed — decanted for the octopus, its dense black fruit and mineral edge opening in the glass the way a landscape opens when clouds part. The instructors fired questions and her answers came from a place that wasn't memory, exactly, but something deeper — understanding that had settled into her the way sediment settles in wine, slowly and then completely.

Ninety-eight percent. Distinction.

She walked out of the building into Barcelona's evening heat and laughed, alone, on the pavement, and didn't care who heard.

---

Rain found her that night in the Gothic Quarter, and she let it.

She walked without destination, hood low, boots splashing through puddles that turned the ancient streets to mirrors. Gargoyles dripped above. Laundry hung heavy on sagging lines between buildings. The air smelled like wet stone and paella rice and the sea, which was always there in Barcelona, even when you couldn't see it — a salt-edge under everything, like a bass note in a song you'd stopped consciously hearing.

She thought about Florence. The girl at Santa Maria Novella with the heavy backpack and the hammering heart. The fumbled buttons on her uniform. The tray of tortellini saved by grit and shaking hands. How impossibly far that felt now — not in time, but in who she'd been.

Florence had been survival. Learning to stand in the heat, to read what a table needed before anyone asked, to turn chaos into something that looked, from the outside, like grace. Barcelona had been acceleration — Quimet's standing-room speed, Disfrutar's millimetre precision, Le Cordon Bleu transforming her palate from eager to expert. And now —

She paused under an archway. Water sheeted off the stone above. The city breathed around her, wet and warm and indifferent to her plans.

And now she needed the next thing. The thing she couldn't quite name yet but could feel, the way you feel a change in weather before the sky shows it.

Her phone buzzed. A classmate: *London intensive this weekend — advanced hospitality seminar at Le Cordon Bleu London. Guest lecturers from Ikoyi. Spots filling. You in?*

Her thumb moved before her brain finished processing. *Booking now.*

The confirmation pinged three minutes later. Ryanair, Friday dawn. Expensive on server wages. Non-negotiable.

---

The farewell happened at Quimet i Quimet on a Friday night, because where else would it happen.

The bar was packed to its usual impossible density — elbows and conservas and bare bulbs and the low roar of Catalan chatter that sounded, after months of immersion, less like a foreign language and more like the particular frequency at which this city vibrated. Quim pulled her into a hug that smelled like olives and sweat and the indefinable scent of a man who'd been standing behind the same bar his grandfather had built and had no intention of standing anywhere else.

"Mi americana con alma española," he said, and pressed a glass of house vermouth into her hand.

She wasn't American, and she wasn't Spanish, and the description was geographically wrong in every direction, and it was the most accurate thing anyone had ever called her.

They commandeered a corner by the window — the crew, the brigade, the family she'd built from standing-room tapas and exam panic and late-night kitchen lessons. Plates piled: montaditos dripping sweet-tart reduction, bombas crisp and potatoey, bravas with the smoky aioli that she'd eaten a hundred times and would remember for the rest of her life.

Stories flew. Her first Quimet shift, when Javier had hip-checked her and she'd saved the tray and he'd pretended it was an accident and everyone knew it wasn't. Maria's midnight calamar lessons, the sizzle of squid on the plancha syncing with their heartbeats. Clara's silent nod at Disfrutar that had felt like receiving a medal.

"London's loss is ours," Quim said, raising his glass. "But you'll conquer it."

She swallowed the lump. Clinked. Drank.

The vermouth was perfect — herbal and bitter and warm — and the people around her were loud and affectionate and slightly drunk, and the bar was too small and too hot and exactly right.

---

Packing came quiet the next dawn. Pale light through gauzy curtains. Suitcase open on the bed.

She folded with the precision she'd learned in kitchens — service pants flat, shirts starched, aprons rolled tight. Le Cordon Bleu notes stacked. Wine certification pinned to the top, the paper already softening at the edges from being handled too many times.

Her sketchbook last.

She sat on the bed and turned the pages. Barcelona's soul in pencil: La Boqueria's kaleidoscope stalls at dawn, pyramids of ruby tomatoes, the octopus vendor whose hands moved like a conductor's. Gothic spires piercing storm clouds. Quimet's bar, hands blurred in mid-gesture, faces she'd drawn from memory because they'd never held still long enough to draw from life.

And the last page — the one she'd added the night before, sitting cross-legged on the bed with the vermouth still warm in her chest. La Boqueria again. The crowd surging. The jamón vendor's knife catching light. And in the background, barely sketched, a figure she'd drawn without quite deciding to: tall, mid-laugh, one hand steady, the other holding something she couldn't see. Drawn from Maria's photograph. Drawn from whatever the photograph had made her feel, which she still couldn't name and had stopped trying to.

She closed the book. Slipped it into the suitcase's front pocket.

---

The airport shuttle rumbled over streets still puddled from the night's rain. Check-in. Security. The indignity of unlacing boots and bagging liquids. Gate area humming with bleary travellers — suited men nursing cortados, families wrangling bags, a solo guitarist tuning softly in a corner, playing something that sounded like it was about leaving and might have been about arriving.

She took a window seat. The plastic armrest was cool under her palm. Engines whined. The taxiway rolled beneath them — cargo bays, blinking beacons, Barcelona's sprawl spreading and then shrinking as the wheels left the tarmac.

Through the window: the Sagrada Família's spires, still reaching, still unfinished, still certain of themselves. The sea a grey-blue smudge beyond the port. Then clouds, and the city was gone.

She pressed her forehead to the cold glass.

In her bag, between the pages of her sketchbook, an oyster shell she'd carried from a London market sat smooth and cool against the paper. She didn't take it out. She didn't need to. She knew it was there the way you know your own heartbeat — constant, unremarkable, fundamental.

London waited. New kitchens. New rhythms. The faint electric tug of something on the horizon that she couldn't see yet but could feel pulling.

Florence's girl had become Barcelona's woman. Whatever came next, she'd meet it with steady hands and eyes that knew what they were looking at.

The plane banked west. Engines humming. Carrying her forward.
