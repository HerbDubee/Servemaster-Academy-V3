The Arno was the color of pewter at this hour, flat and still as a held breath. Sofia's sneakers found the wet cobblestones by memory now — three weeks of the same loop had taught her feet where the street dipped near Santa Croce, where the puddles pooled wide after rain, where the bridge at Ponte alle Grazie offered a half-second of open sky before the buildings closed back in. Dawn came slowly in Florence. It didn't arrive so much as leak in, grey at the edges first, then gold so tentative it seemed unsure of its welcome.

She'd been awake since five. Not from anxiety — something closer to readiness, which felt like a different country from anxiety even when it kept the same hours. Her calves still held the memory of yesterday's close, twelve hours of service knotted into muscle. She pushed harder on the long straight past Santa Croce's facade, let the burn mean something.

The Mercato Centrale woke while she was still a block away. She could hear it before she could see it — crate wheels on uneven stone, metal shutters pulling up in a sound like industry clearing its throat. Inside, the light was warm and specific, pressing down through the iron latticework overhead, and the smell hit her chest the way it always did: bread from the forno below, an almost violent cleanness of citrus from a stack of lemon crates near the entrance, and underneath everything, faint and cold, the metallic edge of fish arriving packed in ice.

Giuseppe's stall was in the corner where the light was best. He didn't look up when she arrived — he never did, not anymore. A pocket knife moved through a wheel of pecorino with a sound like clean wood splitting.

"Buongiorno, Sofia." He set the first slice on waxed paper, began cutting a second.

"Extra figs today, if you have them."

He had them. The figs he chose were nearly too ripe — swollen and dark, skin yielding to the faintest pressure. She ate one standing at his counter while he wrapped the cheese. The sweetness hit first, then the floral bottom note that she'd learned to wait for. A fig that hadn't gotten there yet tasted like nothing. This one tasted like the whole point.

She walked home with juice still on her wrist and didn't think about wiping it.

---

The restaurant on a quiet morning was a different creature entirely.

Sofia had learned this in her first week — how the room transformed between prep and service, the way a theatre transforms when the seats are still empty and the stagehands are working in ordinary clothes. At ten-thirty, Gucci Osteria was all noise and task: pots finding their heat, López calling out specs for the lunch menu, Marco moving through the stations with his clipboard held in the particular way he held it when he was satisfied, which looked identical to how he held it when he wasn't. She'd learned to read the angle of his shoulders instead.

Her own station received her the way a kitchen receives someone who has stopped being watched and started actually working. She checked the silverware for spots in the light, noted two that needed buffing. Refolded a napkin that had been done in haste. Found the small misalignment in the glassware arrangement that no guest would ever notice and corrected it anyway, because correcting it now cost nothing and the attention to it meant something she couldn't quite name but could feel in her hands.

Lunch service hit hard at half twelve. The room filled the way rivers fill — first a trickle, then suddenly everywhere, the space rearranging itself around bodies in motion. Businessmen from Milan who ordered with the efficiency of people accustomed to having their orders taken, a family in the corner that had brought a toddler in good faith and were now paying for that faith in small quiet catastrophes. Sofia worked her section in a rhythm that had taken three weeks to find and now felt, some shifts, nearly effortless.

Nearly.

At the table she'd been watching peripherally since the man sat down — back too straight for comfort, glass held two-fisted, wine nearly gone before the food arrived — she felt the moment sharpen before it broke.

He was somewhere in his mid-forties, jacket too heavy for the season, the bloom of the Barolo bright in his face. His wife had gone quiet in a specific way, the kind of quiet that isn't peace. When Sofia set down the risotto, she saw the man draw breath, and she knew the shape of what was coming before his mouth opened.

"Stone cold." He stabbed a fork into the center of the bowl. "You call this service?"

The wife shifted. Across the table, another couple put their own forks down very carefully, like people stepping out of the way of something.

Sofia stood fully toward him. Not braced — open. She let him have the room.

"When did you notice it?" she asked.

He blinked. "The second bite. It's ruined."

She nodded once, slowly. "Risotto should arrive steaming. I'm sorry it didn't — that's not the meal you should be having." No qualifier. No explanation about kitchen timings, which were true but belonged to her, not to him. "I'll have a fresh one at the table in a few minutes. And I'd like to offer the table an amaro — Nonino, on the house — to settle the meal properly. Does that work?"

The bluster had expected to push against something and found nothing to push against. He deflated slowly, the way over-inflated things do. "The amaro. Fine. Yes."

She got the replacement from the pass, signaled for the digestifs. When she returned and set the new bowl in front of him, the steam was real this time, saffron vivid as a warning. She waited the beat it took for him to take a bite, to chew, to register.

"Better." Grudging, but genuine.

"Good." She topped off the water glasses and moved on.

The wife caught her eye briefly as she passed. It was the smallest acknowledgment — a millimeter of a smile, there and gone. Sofia filed it with everything else the day was teaching her.

---

The afternoon between services had a quality she'd stopped fighting.

The days were long enough in Florence that even after a twelve-table lunch and a twenty-cover dinner, there was light left over. She sat in the small garden on the osteria's side, the one the guests never saw, where a lemon tree grew crookedly toward the wall as if it had somewhere to be. Her sketchbook opened to the middle where the paper was softest from use. She wasn't drawing anything specific — just the ivy curving over the garden wall, the angle of a waiter's silhouette through the glass of the kitchen door, a hand in motion mid-pour caught from memory.

The sketches from her morning runs had accumulated over the weeks: the iron-laced arches of the Mercato, the Ponte Santa Trinita's clean lines, Giuseppe's hands mid-motion with the knife, fingers thick and sure. She didn't try to make them beautiful. She just tried to make them true.

Paolo arrived at ten to seven, which meant he'd been delayed by something. He pushed through the back entrance still pulling his apron strings, grinning like he'd done something to be pleased about. He always looked like that. It was either his best quality or his most dangerous one, depending on the shift.

"Sofia. Tell me you're at my station tonight."

"Marco said station three."

"Good. I need backup." He dropped into the chair beside her, smelling of aftershave and someone else's cigarette smoke. "Vespa?" she asked.

"Broke down by the baptistery. I walked with a nonna who knew three routes to the duomo and insisted on explaining all of them." He spread his hands, completely at peace with this. "What are you going to do."

Maria came through three minutes later — ponytail tight, apron already on, scanning the room the way she always scanned rooms, like she was indexing it. She'd been in Florence three years, and she moved through service with the particular fluency of someone who had made peace with their competence. Sofia had watched her upsell a Barolo at a table of four with the quiet precision of someone defusing something.

"You stayed for debrief yesterday," Maria said to Sofia, not a question.

"López said something about reading hesitation in guests before they signal. I wanted to hear the rest."

Maria nodded. Nothing more. But the nod meant something.

---

The dinner service moved through Sofia like water — she stopped noticing herself in it, which she'd learned was the sign that it was going well. Twenty covers, two difficult tables that resolved themselves, a VIP anniversary at the corner that required the kind of attention that didn't look like attention. She reset the quiet at table seven mid-service — a couple whose conversation had gone somewhere difficult, their body language broadcasting distance across the tablecloth — by arriving with a small dish of amuse-bouche neither of them had ordered, setting it between them without announcement, and stepping away before they could register it as anything other than luck. They were laughing by the time she brought the secondi.

She didn't think about it in those terms while she did it. She only noticed it later, from across the room, the way you notice weather from inside.

Out at half eleven, the three of them biked through the dark streets, the city quieter than it would ever allow itself to be in daylight, the air carrying the particular warmth of old stone releasing the day's heat. The bar near Santa Croce was barely big enough for the stools they needed, the walls yellow with old posters, the wood of the bar worn into something like skin.

Paolo ordered without asking. Three Fernet con coca. He handed them down the bar.

"To Sofia," he said, clinking hard, "who terrorized a businessman today and made him grateful for it."

"He left twenty percent," Maria said, which was not the important thing but somehow was.

Sofia sipped. The bitterness spread through her chest, familiar. Outside, a couple on bicycles passed in the lamplight, tandem, the woman's hand trailing free.

Paolo launched into something that happened in Naples five years ago, a guest who'd proposed mid-pasta, the way the woman's face had moved through six different expressions in three seconds, the way the whole room had held its breath and then exhaled in unison. Maria countered with something from Lima, street carts and chaos and plates that never matched but somehow always held. Sofia told them about avó's kitchen in São Paulo — the wooden spoon on the pot rim, the way a stove that barely worked had fed the whole building without anyone understanding quite how.

What they were doing, she realized — not then, but later, in the quieter version of the same moment that replays in the dark — was telling each other that they came from somewhere. That the work they were doing now was built on something that came before it.

---

The pensione was quiet when she got in. She sat on the bed and looked at the phone for a while before she picked it up.

It rang twice.

"Alô?" The sound of pots. Background television, faint.

"Avó. Sou eu."

The warmth that came back through the signal — crackling, distance-worn, still entirely avó — filled the room up. They talked for twenty minutes in fast Portuguese, Sofia's Italian-soaked tongue readjusting. She described Giuseppe's figs, the businessman, Paolo's vespa. Avó described burning the feijão, which she'd done, she assured Sofia, on purpose, because it added character. Sofia laughed, surprised, the way she always was by avó's particular logic.

"Você está crescendo," avó said finally. Just that, no elaboration. *You're growing.* Not a compliment so much as an observation of something self-evident, the way you'd note that the weather had changed.

"Vou ligar na semana que vem," Sofia said.

"Sim. Vai sim." And the line clicked, and the room was quiet again, but differently.

She opened the sketchbook. Drew Paolo mid-toast, face open, glass raised. Drew Maria's eyes over the rim of hers — precise, warm, watchful. She tried to draw avó's kitchen and couldn't get the light right, the particular orange of late afternoon through slatted blinds. Some things resisted reduction.

Paolo had said something earlier, between stories, almost under his breath — about servers who kept moving. The circuit, he'd called it. Florence to Barcelona, Paris to London, the whole world rearranging itself into kitchens and dining rooms and the particular language you learned to speak in each of them. Maria had nodded. Disfrutar in Barcelona, she said. Quimet i Quimet for something like truth.

Sofia had smiled then and said nothing. But in the dark, with the sketchbook warm on her knees, she felt it: the city outside the city, the next place already pulling at something in her chest, gentle but persistent, the way tides work. Florence wasn't finished with her yet. But she had begun, without deciding to, to listen for what came after.

She put the book away. Set the alarm for five. Lay back in the dark with her hands folded over her stomach, the smell of old stone and someone's laundry from the courtyard drifting in through the window crack, and let Florence do whatever it was doing to her.

It was doing something. She was almost certain.
