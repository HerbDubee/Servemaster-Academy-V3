The Seine was different at 5:15 in October.

Not different the way guidebooks meant — no Monet light, no romantic mist rising from the water in theatrical curls. Different in the way Luca had learned to notice: the sky a flat pewter that hadn't decided yet, the quai cobblestones slick and dark, his own breath visible for the first time since September. The leaves had turned overnight. Not gradually, the way the weather apps kept promising, but in one decisive continental exhale. He ran through them where they'd banked against the stone balustrade, and they scraped against his shoes with a sound like something ending.

Camille's pace matched his. Two meters to his left, always — she'd picked that distance in their first week and maintained it with the quiet precision of someone who'd thought about what she wanted from this arrangement and decided. Her dark hair was flat under her cap. She didn't talk on runs, which was one of the things he liked about her. Neither did he.

At the turnaround she pulled even. "Pace holding?"

"Yes."

Her breath was even despite the hill. She scanned the water traffic ahead — a barge moving slow under Pont de Sully, someone on the deck smoking in the dark. Then she dropped back to her two meters and they ran home.

Le Gabriel occupied a space that seemed to absorb noise rather than contain it. Velvet drapes, chandeliers throwing prisms onto mahogany, the ceiling high enough that whispers simply disappeared. Luca had spent his first two shifts cataloguing the differences from Cheval Blanc — the intimacy here wasn't incidental, it was constructed, every element calibrated to make a guest feel that the room existed for them specifically, that nothing beyond those drapes was quite real.

Twenty-eight covers maximum. Fewer than half the Plénitude count.

He'd recalibrated accordingly.

His notebook had changed. At Plénitude it held wine temperatures, decanting times, the technical architecture of a meal. Here it held people. Monsieur Duval: table seven, Thursdays, always the sole meunière, Sancerre 2018 and nothing with oak, ever. Madame Lefèvre: anniversaries, no cilantro, a sadness around her eyes that arrived before her husband and stayed after he'd started talking. The notebook grew thick with these — fragments of lives he was trusted with, briefly, across a white tablecloth. He kept it in the breast pocket of his jacket. It sat warm against his chest through service.

The knowledge was the thing he hadn't expected. Not just the utility of it — though that was real, and satisfying in the way that any precision tool was satisfying when it worked correctly. But the weight. Learning someone that carefully meant carrying them a little. Monsieur Henriot, for instance: the widower who came every Thursday at eight, sat at the same table without being told, ordered the same cut of beef with the same slight specification about the edge. Mid-sixties, silver hair impeccable, suit that had been made for him a decade ago and still fit. He wore a locket. Small, oval. In it was a photograph that had begun, at the edges, to fade.

Luca had gotten most of it from Raoul — Raoul, who had the salt-and-pepper temples and the encyclopedic memory of someone who'd been watching this particular room for nine years. "The wife died eighteen months ago," Raoul had said, not unkindly, reviewing the reservation flag one afternoon before service. "Chassagne-Montrachet 2014. That was hers. He's never ordered it."

"Has he looked at it on the list?"

Raoul considered. "Last week. Just for a moment."

The room hummed at full service, the kind of controlled velocity that looked effortless only from the outside. Banctel's kitchen sent out hay-smoked eel that you could smell arriving three tables away, wild duck with fig leaf, a chocolate ganache with passionfruit underneath it like a secret. Luca moved through it all with the practiced invisibility he'd been refining since Plénitude — present without obtruding, attentive without performing attention. He read micro-movements: the man at table two adjusting his cufflinks every four minutes, the couple at eleven whose laughter had been getting louder and more effortful since the fish course.

Monsieur Henriot arrived at 20:02. He settled into his chair, unfolded his napkin, and placed both hands flat on the table in a gesture that Luca had come to recognize as the way a person anchors themselves when they are very much alone but intend to manage it with dignity.

Luca gave him the amuse-bouche. Let the room find its rhythm. Then brought the wine list — not opened, just presented — and crouched to the table's level so they were eye to eye.

"Bonsoir, Monsieur Henriot. I've been thinking about Chassagne-Montrachet."

Not a question. The bottle had already been retrieved.

Something crossed the man's face — surprise first, then a reflexive wariness, and then something underneath both of those that Luca couldn't quite categorize. He held the bottle horizontally, label outward, and said nothing else.

Henriot looked at the label for a long moment.

"She would have loved this room," he said finally. His voice was steady, very nearly composed. "She loved rooms like this. Rooms that took care with things."

"Then it should be poured," Luca said.

He uncorked it with the quietest possible motion — barely a sigh from the cork, no theater. Poured clockwise from the right, measured and exact, the twist-and-sever at the end leaving the rim clean. The glass sat at thirty degrees to the place setting, where it caught the candlelight and held it.

Henriot swirled. Inhaled. Closed his eyes for the duration of one breath.

When he opened them there was a tear tracking one cheek, and he made no move to hide it. He simply sat with it the way you sit with weather.

"Thank you, Luca."

"Of course."

Luca stepped back two paces and let the moment be what it was. His chest had done something unexpected in the past thirty seconds — a tightening that had nothing to do with the technical execution, which had been flawless. He had delivered the meal correctly. And the correctness had moved through some membrane he hadn't known existed and become something else entirely.

He filed it, the way he filed everything. But the filing felt different from the others.

The Barcelona stage was a one-day thing — Banctel's request, a relationship with a supplier, someone who needed watching for an Iberian pivot. Luca took the early flight, landed before La Boqueria had fully opened, walked the market as it assembled itself around him.

The stalls ran loud. Fish guts and paprika, crates of citrus, vendors barking across each other in Catalan. A man with no visible front teeth was selling something from a cooler and declaring its virtues to no one in particular. A child had positioned herself at the entrance to eat a chocolate-covered strawberry with the focused seriousness of someone participating in a solemn ceremony.

Luca queued for jamón — pata negra, acorn-fed, the slices falling from the blade in tissue-thin sheets — and watched the stall across from him where a woman was weighing saffron threads into a small paper envelope with the gravity of a pharmacist.

Two stalls farther down: a laugh.

It cut through the market noise like a single note through an orchestra going too loud — warm, bright, Portuguese-accented Spanish, the words themselves lost in the crowd's din but the voice underneath them absolutely clear. Something in it caught. Not the way beautiful things caught you in Paris, with a vague pleasurable ache. This was something more precise. Like a string plucked that another string, distant, responds to.

He turned.

Crates tumbling — a tourist's bag caught on a corner, oranges scattering across the concrete, bodies surging to avoid them, a vendor shouting. The crowd swelled and closed. He caught a glimpse of dark curls moving toward the Gothic arches, a paper saffron pouch clutched in one hand, and then she was swallowed by the bodies and the light and the chaos of a market that didn't pause for anyone.

He stood there for a moment with the jamón in his hand.

His pulse was doing something it had no business doing after a queue for cured meat.

He walked to the stage. Quimet i Quimet: a room the size of a large closet, no reservations, counter service, the bar lined with tins stacked floor to ceiling and every inch of standing space occupied by lunch-hour regulars who'd been claiming the same square meter of floor since before he was born. The service ran on instinct and muscle memory — the owner's, not his, which meant Luca's job was to observe and not impede, which was harder than it sounded. He was good at observation. He was less good at making peace with being the least capable person in the room.

But the laugh came back to him between bites of conserva, between sips of vermouth. Something about it had been familiar in a way he couldn't categorize. He had, he realized, added a new folder to his mental system. Label not yet assigned. The folder held a silhouette on a Paris bridge, a laugh in a rainstorm at the Luxembourg, a voice in a Barcelona market he'd never heard clearly enough to recognize again.

He filed the Barcelona variable under it and caught the evening flight home.

---

Thursday morning run. Camille pulling even at the turnaround, the river the same pewter it had been all month.

"Barcelona?"

"Productive."

She glanced sideways at him. "You look like you processed something that didn't process cleanly."

He thought about this. "A guest cried," he said finally. "The good way. I didn't expect to carry it."

She nodded, the way she nodded when something made sense to her that she didn't feel the need to expand on. They ran the rest of the way in silence, which was the thing he liked best about her — the absence of the need to explain.

Home. Espresso. His notebook open on the kitchen table, Positano lemons on the wall above it, Camille's photograph small in its frame beside them.

He updated the Henriot entry: the wine, the tear, the phrase she would have loved this room. Then sat with his pen above a fresh line.

In the margin, very small, he wrote: *La Boqueria, midday. A voice.*

He stared at it. Then left it as it was.

London's Cordon Bleu enrollment confirmation sat on the corkboard. Ikoyi whispered next — Jeremy Chan's kitchen, West African precision, a different kind of fire than any he'd trained in. Paris was deepening, winter coming, the leaves finished with their turning. He had four weeks left.

The systems said nothing about what you took with you when a chapter ended.

He drank his coffee. The lemons on the wall glowed in the early light like a thing already remembered.
