Chapter 2 — Paris Precision

The Seine at five in the morning was a different river. No gold, no tourists, no broken light worth sketching. Just a black current moving with the quiet indifference of something ancient and unconvinced, the embankment lamps still burning amber over water that didn't need the light. Luca ran the quai de la Mégisserie in the dark, breath clouding, footsteps on cobblestone the only sound he was responsible for. A barge moved somewhere out there — invisible, just engine noise and the low slosh of water separating from stone.

He ran without music. He always had. Music imposed a rhythm that wasn't his, and his was already precise enough without borrowing someone else's. Four ten per kilometer, heart rate climbing steady. The body a system under careful management. His father had believed that — Klaus Voss, Munich civil engineer, a man who timed his sons' homework and checked the calibration on the kitchen clock each spring. Luca had inherited the precision the way other people inherited eye color: visibly, indelibly, without having asked for it. He'd spent most of his teenage years equal parts grateful and mildly horrified by this fact.

What he'd added was the watching.

That came from his mother's side. Rosaria Voss née Ferrante, Amalfi-born, who could read a stranger's mood from the other end of a trattoria terrace and have the right thing — a glass of water, a breadstick, a moment of pointed silence — in place before they'd formed the thought themselves. You learn or you don't, she'd say, which wasn't really teaching at all. It was just true. What she never told him was what to do with the learning once you had it, how to carry the weight of other people's states without letting it hollow you out. He was still working on that part.

He hit the turnaround point near Notre-Dame's scaffolding and held for thirty seconds, checking. Pulse: the right kind of elevated. The sky over the île de la Cité had begun its long slow lightening, not quite pink, just a lessening of dark. A street sweeper moved along the far bank, broom rasping in a rhythm that matched nothing. Luca watched him for a moment — the man's particular forward lean, the practiced arc — and felt something he couldn't name, some small stab of recognition. Then he pushed homeward.

The Marais apartment was fifteen square meters of deliberate economy. He showered, dressed, and was on the Vélib' by six-fifteen, pedaling south in the thin blue morning.

Three months in Paris and the croissant calibration had become ritual — not indulgence exactly, not even pleasure in the way people used the word. More like tuning an instrument before you played it. Du Pain et des Idées on rue Yves Toudic: first in line most mornings, the baker acknowledging him with a nod that said I know what you want with the efficiency of a man who'd filed Luca under the quiet German, escargot pistache, doesn't make conversation. Which was accurate, if reductive. Luca appreciated him for it.

The thing about the croissant ritual that he'd never told anyone, because it would sound insane, was

that he graded them. Not formally. Just internally. The lamination, the pull, the place where the steam escaped and the crust gave — these things varied day to day in ways so fine they were almost imperceptible and also, to Luca, completely obvious. Today's was a nine out of ten. Yesterday's had been an eight, and he'd spent ten minutes after trying to identify why and hadn't managed it, which bothered him more than he wanted to admit.

He ate across the street at a zinc counter, watching the early commuters — delivery riders, a nurse just off shift with her lanyard still swinging, two concierges comparing last night's complaints in rapid Parisian shorthand. He caught a fragment: — six times, six, I said to him, monsieur the floor does not vacuum itself — and the other one laughing in the sharp honest way of a person who has had this exact problem.

He noticed what he always noticed: who was tired, who was preoccupied, who needed more space and who needed acknowledgment. It happened without decision, the way breathing happened. He'd tried to explain it to a woman he'd briefly dated in Munich — this constant ambient reading of rooms — and she'd said that sounds exhausting and he'd realized she was right, that it was, and also that he couldn't stop. What he hadn't told her, because he'd only understood it later on a train to Lyon, was that the exhaustion was half the point. The watching kept him from having to watch himself too closely.

He finished the croissant. Nine out of ten. He biked to work.

Plénitude occupied its corner of the Cheval Blanc like a kept secret — three Michelin stars tucked behind a glass facade that looked over the Seine with the slightly smug satisfaction of something that knows its own quality. Arnaud Donckele's kitchen produced the kind of food that required a dining room to receive it properly: linens starched, glassware polished to the point of almost not existing, decanters aligned like a quiet argument about patience.

Luca had been on the floor here for three months, transferred from a stage in Munich that had taught him everything about structure and nothing about warmth. Plénitude was teaching him refinement. There was a difference — he understood it in theory, was still finding it in practice. Marie was already at the station when he arrived at eight, folding napkins into bishops' mitres with the focused efficiency of someone who genuinely liked the work. She was twenty-four, recently out of a hotel school in Lyon, and she laughed at things that weren't funny to indicate she found them interesting. It had taken Luca three weeks to decode this. He'd spent the first week thinking she was the most easily amused person in Paris, the second week wondering if the joke was always on him, and the third week finally recognizing it as a social mechanism, a way of saying I'm paying attention, go on. Once he understood that, he found he liked her considerably more.

"Timing on the caviar service?" he asked, scanning the evening's reservation sheet. "Table six at fourteen-twelve," Marie said, hands still moving. She glanced up. "They pushed from earlier. Apparently the wife wanted to arrive by train and the train had opinions about that." "Confirm with kitchen at thirteen-fifty." He studied the book. Twelve tables, peak at

twenty-forty-five.

Jacques looked up from across the room, where he was moving through his mise en place with the economy of a man who'd done it ten thousand times and expected to do it ten thousand more. He had veteran energy, slightly haunted around the eyes. At one point Luca had tried to ask him about his early career and Jacques had produced a silence so thorough that Luca had never tried again. "Laurent at table nine," Jacques said, not looking up. "Know the name?"

"Bordeaux. Tech money, Lyon."

"Also a man who will tell you what wine he wants and then be annoyed if you agree too quickly." Jacques set a fork down with the particular care of someone who had learned this lesson expensively. "Don't just take the order. Give him the weight of it."

Marie laughed — the real kind this time, not the social one. Luca had learned to tell them apart. "That's almost wise, Jacques. Did you sleep?"

"Never," Jacques said, without humor, and returned to his work.

Luca walked the dining space once, slowly, like a pianist running scales. The tables said nothing yet — white cloth, stillness, the particular potential of empty chairs. Table nine was in the warm corner, candlelight favorable, a good position for a man who liked to set pace. He could see the whole room from there, which men like Laurent always preferred, whether or not they knew they preferred it. By nineteen hundred it would be a different room entirely, full of the small private dramas that restaurants existed to host. He liked this hour, though. The room before the room. Wife: quiet elegance, earrings catching the light, the practiced ease of someone who'd been to many of these dinners and had learned to find her own pleasure in them regardless of what the men at the table were doing. Sato-san from Tokyo took his seat last, menu in hand immediately, studying it with the careful attention of someone who understood that menus contained information if you knew how to read them. He and Luca were going to get along, Luca thought, and then realized he was assigning the man a personality from four gestures, which was something he did constantly and tried not to do. Luca approached at twenty-twenty-two, just past the amuse-bouche — enough time to settle in, not so much time to feel unattended.

"Bonsoir." He laid the wine list between them, angled so all three could see without craning. Brief introductions. Laurent waved off the list almost before Luca had finished placing it, which was either confidence or impatience, both of which required the same response.

"The '98 Lafite. Decanted."

A good choice, well-made, appropriate to the evening. Jacques had been right — Luca let a beat of silence pass before he said anything, giving the selection the air of a thing being properly received. "Of course, Monsieur." He signaled Marie without looking, and the bottle arrived from the cradle the way the best things arrived: quietly, as though it had simply been there waiting. He presented it horizontally, label in the light, and watched Laurent's face do the small private thing faces did when they recognized something they'd wanted. The confirmation came in a nod that had the self-satisfaction of someone whose good taste had just been validated.

The uncorking produced no sound worth commenting on. The pour: clockwise from the right, Madame first — garnet in crystal, catching the dining room's low light and doing something richer with it. Sato-san next. Laurent last, the host's due. A pass of linen over the bottle's lip, unhurried, and retreat.

Laurent swirled, considered, tasted. The nod came.

At twenty-one-oh-three, kitchen sent a ping. Pigeon delayed, two minutes.

Two minutes was nothing. Two minutes was also, at this table, at this point in an evening, enough time for the air to shift into something uncertain. Luca had noticed Sato-san's micro-glance at his watch already — barely there, a fraction — and Madame's hand moving to her necklace with the particular restlessness of someone waiting without quite admitting it. Laurent was still talking, but the rhythm of his sentences had changed slightly. He was aware.

Luca was at the table in twenty seconds.

"Monsieur Laurent." He kept his voice low, unhurried. "If I may — a bridge. The 2015 Clos de Tart, a Morey-Saint-Denis. Something to carry you through."

Laurent set down his glass and looked at him with the evaluating patience of a man who was used to proposals. "Morey-Saint-Denis."

"Clos de Tart specifically. It's a Grand Cru — monopole. Violet and earth, nothing heavy. In these glasses, tonight, it will be exactly right."

A pause. Laurent's eyes moved to his wife, the question small and wordless. She didn't respond visibly, but something in her posture said why not.

"You're suggesting a Burgundy after a Bordeaux," Laurent said.

"After a Lafite, yes. The Clos de Tart won't compete — it'll pivot. It's built differently." Luca found himself wanting to explain it properly, not as a sales script but as the actual thing he meant. "The Lafite has architecture. The Clos de Tart has weather. Between courses, you want weather." Laurent stared at him for a moment with an expression Luca couldn't read, which was rare. Then he said: "That's a ridiculous distinction and I know exactly what you mean. Pour." The Burgundy bloomed in the glasses — deeper ruby than the Lafite, something in it that wasn't quite restless but wasn't settled either, the way afternoon thunderstorms felt right before they arrived.

Sato-san inhaled once, slowly, without comment, and the set of his shoulders changed. The wife smiled — her first unguarded one of the evening. The delay became invisible.

Luca retreated to his position. And here was the thing he noted, filed, tried to understand: the two minutes hadn't disappeared. The pigeon was still late. He hadn't fixed the delay. He'd given the table somewhere else to be while they waited, and they'd gone there willingly, and now they were better off than they'd been before the problem arose. That was different from solving a problem. That was something else.

He stood very still for a moment, thinking about it. Warmth and precision weren't opposites. But warmth was the thing you reached for when precision ran out of road.

He thought about Nonna, who had never once in her life presented a wine list, but who had always known when to bring the grappa. He thought about his father, who would have found a technical solution to the two-minute delay and provided it clinically and wondered why the table seemed unmoved.

He didn't know what to call what he'd just done. He'd work it out later.

The service ended at half past ten. Debrief in the back: a grunt of approval from Donckele, a clap on the shoulder from the chef de salle. Numbers reviewed — wines upsold, zero errors, table nine's tip projection solid. Everything in its proper column.

In the corridor afterward, Marie caught up with him while he was changing his jacket. "The bridge wine," she said. "Table nine. That was nice."

"It worked."

"It was more than worked." She considered him with the look she used when she was actually assessing something, not the social-laugh mode but the real attention underneath it. "Where'd you learn that?"

Luca thought about it. "I don't know. Tonight, maybe."

Marie looked at him for a second, then laughed — the real kind. "God, you're annoying," she said, not unkindly, and walked off.

Jacques was pulling on his coat by the back door. He'd overheard. He said, without looking up: "Clos de Tart. Good instinct."

"Laurent called it a ridiculous distinction."

"Did he like the wine?"

"Yes."

Jacques nodded once, coat buttoned. "Then the distinction worked." He pushed through the door into the alley. A pause, and then, from somewhere in the dark: "The technical solution is the easy part, Voss. Anyone can do the technical solution."

The door swung shut.

Luca stood in the corridor for a moment in the particular quiet of a kitchen after service, the smell of stock and citrus and hot metal already dissipating. He thought he understood what Jacques meant. He wasn't entirely sure he'd earned it yet.

He walked out just before one, the Seine to his left, running quiet under the bridges. The streets had the specific quality of Paris after midnight — a loosening, something that had been held in by day now allowed to be a little more itself. Restaurant terraces mostly dark. A few wine bars still open, laughter spilling through frosted glass.

He stopped at an unmarked place in the 11th that he'd found by accident, the kind of bar that didn't advertise because its customers didn't need to be told. The bartender — scarred hands, the face of a man who'd heard most things and stopped being surprised by any of them — slid an espresso across without being asked. Luca wrapped both hands around the cup.

Across the room, a couple two stools down were arguing in the gentle low way of two people who already knew how the argument would end and were just finding their way back through it. Luca caught a word here and there — toujours, mais là, something that might have been a name — and watched without watching. The man's face turned slightly away, not refusing contact, just needing a moment. The woman's hand rested on his arm, not insisting, just there.

The bartender appeared at Luca's elbow, refilling the espresso with the unhurried authority of a man on his own territory.

Dure nuit? he asked.

Mais victorieuse, Luca said.

The bartender considered this with the philosophical patience of someone who'd heard both better and worse answers at one in the morning. He nodded slowly, as though filing it. Across the room, the man exhaled. The woman moved her thumb a fraction of an inch — barely there, a small signal sent in a private language — and something in the room eased. They didn't speak for a moment. They didn't need to.

Luca felt something in his chest, some loosening he hadn't expected. He looked into the espresso. He thought: that's the thing, isn't it. You can't engineer that. You can only be present when it happens. He wasn't sure what to do with this thought. He was fairly certain it was important. He biked home through the Marais, past the closed shops and the sodium light, past a cat observing him from a windowsill with an expression of such complete philosophical indifference that for a moment he envied it. The cat blinked once, slowly, and returned to its private concerns. Luca felt, irrationally, judged. He also felt, slightly less irrationally, that the cat had a point about the value of extended resting.

The apartment waited: dark, fifteen square meters, efficient. The photo of Nonna's lemon grove on the wall — taken in summer, the trees heavy, the light going gold. He'd put it there on his first night

and occasionally forgot it existed and then looked up and felt it like a hand on the shoulder. He opened his notebook before bed. He wrote: Clos de Tart, 21:06. Architecture vs. weather. Then he sat with the pen in his hand for a moment, thinking about the couple at the bar, about the woman's thumb and the thing that had shifted. He thought about Jacques in the alley: the technical solution is the easy part.

He understood, he thought, what all of it was telling him. He'd spent his whole life building precision as a way to be useful to people, and precision was real and necessary and it would take him places. But precision was a language for describing a room. It wasn't the same as being in it. He wrote: Precision and warmth. Not opposites. But warmth is what you use when precision runs out of room.

He looked at it for a second, crossed out room, wrote road.

Then he closed the notebook and went to sleep.

In the morning he would run the quai again. He would eat the same croissant at the same counter — aiming for ten out of ten, resigned to nine — and he would arrive at Plénitude eight minutes before prep and the room would be empty and still. And there was something waiting in all of that, not boredom, not drift, but a question he couldn't quite form. He'd heard from Bruno Verjus's network that they were looking at trial shifts. Table, in the 12th. Two stars, twelve covers, a philosophy built on ingredient obsession that was almost the opposite of Plénitude's architectural perfection. He lay in the dark and thought about the couple at the bar. About the systems that made rooms run and the small human things that actually mattered inside them. About Laurent saying that's a ridiculous distinction and I know exactly what you mean. About Sato-san's shoulders changing when the wine arrived.

The Seine was still out there moving. It didn't care about any of this. Luca found that, strangely, comforting.

He turned off the light.
