Chapter 10 — Decision Cutoff / Final Singapore Evenings

By the turn of the year, Singapore had stopped pretending to be temporary.

It had become a city of numbered evenings.

Luca felt that first in the flat, not on the floor. Lists had taken over the kitchenette. Sydney had become a printed address, a transit route, a note about the first family meal, a warning not to trust airport adaptors or his own arrival-day judgment. Sofia's Buenos Aires move had narrowed into categories too — documents, first-night clothes, hotel stopgap, luggage transfer, key handoff, the first forty-eight hours too close now to be romanticized. They still drank coffee standing up before service. They still moved around each other with the ease Singapore had made dangerous. But the shape of their mornings had changed.

Nothing was indefinite now.

New Year's Eve arrived with a Wong family lunch complicated enough to remind Luca that rich tables and future moves were built on the same weakness. Too many people wanting to keep options open. Too many voices wanting equal weight. The room did not need more flexibility. It needed the right center.

He found it in the mother before the second course. She was the still point the rest of the table was unconsciously timing itself against — her posture, the slight pause before she lifted her glass, the way the son went quiet when she spoke. Not through authority. Through the particular gravity of the person who had the least need to perform. Luca had seen this before at other tables: the room's actual leader was almost never the loudest voice. She was the person whose stillness other people unconsciously synchronized to without knowing it. He protected her pace from the son's impatience, fielded the nephew's questions before they reached her, kept the table from fracturing into opinions dressed as hospitality, and wrote the principle down afterward because that was what he did when a day insisted on being useful.

Find the person the table is actually following.

The line stayed with him — less for what it said about service and more for what it had started to say about him. He knew, now, what his own attention was following.

That was the problem.

On New Year's Day, Sofia sat cross-legged on her bed with an open suitcase and the expression she reserved for logistics that had become insulting by being correct.

"What category?" Luca asked from the doorway.

"Hostile competence," she said, holding up her phone. "Buenos Aires advisory council."

He crossed the room and read over her shoulder. Carry-on. Checked bag. Buy there. Do not let departure week become a referendum on identity. Do not pack for an imaginary future self. He gave the phone back. "Cruel. Accurate."

"That is not comforting."

"No," he said. "It is January."

She looked at the suitcase. "What if I pack badly and begin an entire new city from an avoidable mistake?"

He considered that with enough seriousness to annoy her. "Passport is catastrophe. Wrong extra sweater is commerce. Packing for a symbolic version of yourself is literature." She laughed despite herself.

That was how the week kept moving. Not through confession. Through categories. Through the mercy of reducing fear into sequence before it could become theater. They built piles on the counter — first-shift clothes, tools, things to buy later, things that only looked necessary because leaving made ordinary objects sentimental. By the time they left for service, the suitcase had become finite again. That was as close to kindness as either of them trusted.

Friday morning turned his own leaving into paperwork.

Sydney on one side of the counter. Odette exit on the other. Locker clear-out. Uniform return. Regular-guest notes. The human handoff that lived nowhere official and would absolutely matter the week after he left.

Sofia read the page over his shoulder. "Make two versions. One formal enough for management. One human enough for the floor."

"That sounds like double work."

"It sounds like not hiding useful truth inside official language."

He hated how quickly she was right.

So he sorted his knowledge the way she forced him to sort everything lately: sacred, useful, vanity, and — after she took the pen from his hand — escalate if. Which regulars actually needed their pace checked instead of merely saying they trusted the room. Which ones wanted continuity and which only wanted the feeling of being remembered. Which details mattered after he left and which only flattered his ego because they proved he had once been necessary.

She crossed out a note about the direction of a napkin crease.

"Decorative knowledge," she said.

Then she wrote escalate if at the bottom of the page, and the whole document suddenly had a spine.

He looked at the four categories for a moment before she capped the pen. Sacred, useful, vanity, escalate if. He thought — not aloud, he kept it as a notation — This is a scoring rubric. Something about it felt larger than the handoff document. He didn't say it. He wrote it in his notebook after she went to change for service. One line, no explanation. He wasn't sure yet why it mattered. He looked at her for one beat too long after the pen went down.

"You make structures ruder and more useful," he said.

"One of my several charms," she said.

That same day, the dining room looked calm enough to lie. Post-holiday lunch. Flat linen. Polished glass. The particular composed stillness of a city glad the calendar had moved on. A room that suggested spare capacity it did not actually possess. Luca took a Goh family table that mistook composure for elasticity and kept them from promising themselves a lunch the kitchen could no longer still deliver well. It was good work. Clean work. Necessary.

It also left him with the more private recognition that he and Sofia had started doing the same thing with time. They were calling it discussion because neither of them yet wanted to call it a deadline. Saturday sharpened the lesson.

He woke with Sydney's changed Friday call time on his phone and Nia's message beneath it: do not confuse a clean handoff with your own continued importance.

So he built the Bell notes carefully. Facts. Cues. Sequence. Escalation. No sentiment dressed as information.

When Claire pulled him to another table mid-lunch, he handed the Bell table to Sofia and made himself walk away. He got three steps toward the other table and felt the particular pull of not turning back — the need to hover at the edge of his own peripheral vision and confirm she was handling it correctly. The hardest part of a real handoff wasn't the trust. He trusted Sofia without question. The harder thing was understanding that trust wasn't the issue. The issue was watching someone else make the table their own and finding yourself hoping, briefly and wrongly, that they might do it in a way that still resembled you. That was vanity dressed as quality concern. He recognized it for what it was and kept walking.

He kept walking.

Across the room, at the edge of his awareness, he could sense her presence at the Bell table without looking. He looked once, briefly, at the wrong moment — she was already mid-sentence with the elder Mrs. Bell, posture relaxed, not imitating him, not performing continuity. Just serving the table. He turned back to his own work.

When he returned, Iris Bell said, with the cold precision of a woman who had seen many teams mistake sentiment for standards, "Your colleague did not perform your absence. That was intelligent."

The silence afterward lasted one beat. Not long. Enough.

Sofia relayed the line later while lifting coffee cups between tables.

"He said 'as usual' again," she told him.

"And?"

"I told him we'd preserved the part of usual that actually loved him."

He stared at her.

"That is a terrifyingly good sentence."

"Yes," she said. "I stole it from being me."

She moved on to the next table before he had found anything worth adding.

Then William Daniels arrived for late lunch and, with the maddening economy of men who never wasted a sentence, said what Luca had already started to fear.

Most people, William told him, claimed they wanted strong systems and then quietly built them around one indispensable person. Everyone called that excellence until the person left and the whole place began apologizing for itself. Love and leadership, he added on the way out, were usually not proved by intensity. They were proved by what grew steadier in your absence. Luca carried that line all the way home.

When William reached the door, he turned briefly and said something about the wine selection, something polite and forgettable, and then he was gone. Luca stood with the menu in his hand for a moment after. The line had arrived too accurately to be accidental. The kind of sentence that a certain kind of person delivered once, to exactly one person, at exactly the right moment, and then walked out as if they had only been discussing the fish.

So did Sofia, though he suspected she would have denied it if asked.

They walked by the river after close because walking had become the city's cleanest way of letting them think beside each other without turning thought into a spectacle. Boat Quay was too noisy — the last of the holiday crowds, people performing celebration at full volume — so they kept going until the lights widened and the water began reflecting the skyline back in long, indifferent lines. Singapore at night in early January: post-holiday cool, the air unusually still, the usual faint smell of the harbor mixed with something from the food stalls closing down nearby. The city didn't soften. It just got quieter and more precise, which was worse.

That was when Sofia gave him the real version of Buenos Aires.

Not the practical summary. The emotional offense.

Hotel for two nights. Luggage transferred later. Key handoff delayed. Nothing unsafe. Nothing fatal. Just ugly enough to ruin the first arrival she had wanted to make elegant. The particular indignity of a

beginning that refused to begin properly — that made the first forty-eight hours in a new city feel like an administrative error rather than a start. She had planned this move with the precision she applied to everything, and the city was going to receive her with a logistics gap anyway, politely, as if competence had nothing to do with it.

He listened without interrupting. The tour boat passed at some point — full of people enjoying themselves loudly enough to seem contractual — and neither of them looked at it. When she finished, he said, "So the first forty-eight hours are untidy. Not unsafe." She cut him a look. "Need you narrate me so accurately?"

"Apparently."

They kept walking.

"It's getting close now," she said.

He did not insult her by asking what it meant.

"Yes."

"Monday after next, I'm in Buenos Aires."

"Thursday, I'm in Sydney."

The dates landed harder spoken aloud than they had in messages and notebooks. They sounded less like plans than objects now. Things you could run into in a corridor.

Sofia watched the river. "I thought if I kept the logistics neat enough, maybe the leaving part would stay abstract a little longer."

"Did it work?" he asked.

"No."

"Pity."

She smiled without humor. "You?"

"No."

That should have made the night sad. Instead it made it exact.

A few steps later she said, quieter now, "You make it difficult to keep things superficial." He looked at her properly then.

"I'm not trying to keep them superficial," he said.

No one reached for the other. No one broke the shape they had kept this long. But something shifted anyway — not in what was said but in the quality of what was no longer pretending to be unsaid. By the time they turned back toward City Hall, the week no longer felt like something they were

observing from professional distance.

It felt scheduled.

By Monday morning, Sydney was no longer a rumor.

Nathan confirmed the Friday floor call. Nia followed almost immediately with the sentence Luca deserved.

Decide today what you're doing with the last three evenings in Singapore. Stop behaving like time will politely widen because you are competent.

He sat on the edge of the bed and wrote his list.

must happen today: confirm uniform / shoes / route. separate pack / ship / leave. finish cellar handoff for Claire. stop pretending there is an indefinite future version of Singapore. He hated the last line on sight. Which was how he knew it was useful.

In the kitchenette, Sofia was reading a message from Buenos Aires about whether she wanted still or sparkling water in the staff-housing welcome basket and reacting as if luxury were a personal insult. "Sparkling," he said after hearing the problem. "You enjoy resenting it and drinking it anyway." She looked up. "Unpleasantly accurate."

She took the notebook from his hand and read the page. When she reached the last line, she did not soften it.

"Good," she said.

"Good?"

"Yes. Because it's true. And because you've been acting for two days like if you remain sufficiently excellent at ordinary tasks, the week won't become specific."

That would have annoyed him less if it had been even slightly wrong.

He leaned against the counter. "Three evenings is not much structure to work with." "That depends what you're trying to avoid calling structure," she said.

He laughed, because otherwise he would have had to admit she had the whole chapter pinned. Then he said, "Sydney is confirmed. Friday at nine."

She nodded once. "Good."

The word carried relief and injury together.

So did the day.

At lunch, Claire handed him a Mercer family table already sick with a particular kind of indecision.

Anniversary parents. Adult children. Shellfish questions circling without landing. A vegetarian maybe. A gallery appointment after — maybe, possibly, depending. A duck decision that would stop being a real option if the kitchen had to hold much longer.

He let them run. He watched.

For the first twenty minutes he didn't press. He listened to how they looped — not genuinely weighing options, but doing the thing that people with comfortable resources sometimes did, which was treat the restaurant as a space that would hold itself open indefinitely at their pace. The confusion wasn't stupidity and it wasn't rudeness. It was a particular stalling behavior: the belief that keeping options alive was the same as keeping quality alive. That leaving things open was a form of care rather than a form of delay. They thought they were being careful. They were actually watching three good things slowly downgrade into adequate ones while they deliberated.

He felt the exact second at which discussion became decay. There was a specific texture to it — the third time the father returned to the shellfish question, the daughter's eyes went to her phone briefly then away, the mother's posture shifted almost imperceptibly from engaged to resigned. The table had crossed from considering to circling.

He did not dress it up.

"I can keep three excellent options alive for you for about one more minute," he said, evenly. "After that, two of them become stories you almost had."

The line worked because it arrived as a diagnosis, not a pressure. He gave them the remaining good options with the calm of someone reporting facts. The mother chose the shorter route. The children chose reality. The father submitted with theatrical disappointment and real relief. The table left on time for their very real gallery appointment, slightly astonished and rather pleased about it. Claire made him write the lesson on the back of the beverage order sheet before the next cover arrived.

If you need the answer tonight, stop calling it an ongoing discussion.

Sofia added beneath it, in her smaller, sharper hand:

Clarity reduces pressure when the room already knows time is real.

Claire read both lines and said, "Useful. Ugly. Keep it. And if it applies to anything outside table ten, be adult about it before departure turns you sentimental and tedious."

No one looked at anyone for a full second.

That was the closest thing to mercy available.

Dinner ran clean enough to leave thought alive, which was the danger.

By 9:43, Luca had done every task that could be done without requiring courage. Shirts packed. Route saved offline. Reimbursement question sent. Sydney paperwork filed. The administrative half

of him had reached the edge of its usefulness and was now simply creating the appearance of occupation.

Sofia found him in the corridor outside the dining room — narrow, dim, the low refrigeration hum behind the service doors, stacked porcelain glowing faintly on the shelving unit against the wall, the smell of fresh linen from the press closet two doors down. The particular quiet of a restaurant after close: all the energy of service still present in the air like a warm room that has only just emptied. He had his phone in his hand. His face was, apparently, too readable to hide behind linen inventories. "What now?" she asked.

"Nothing dramatic."

"Then it's probably important."

He looked at her. She looked back at him with the steadiness Singapore had made between them over six months — not the steadiness of distance, but the kind built by two people who had been in close quarters through enough pressure to know exactly where the other person's honesty lived. There was no one there except the refrigeration hum and the polished backs of service doors. No reason left, suddenly, to pretend that later was a place that still had room in it. "I was trying to decide whether to ask you something tonight or later," he said. She set down the tray stand she was carrying very carefully. "Later when?"

The whole week narrowed to that. Not accusation. Not invitation yet. Just the clean refusal to let him hide inside a vaguer word. When was a diagnosis, not a question. It said: you know there is no later that means what you're using it to mean.

He looked at her and understood, with the kind of clarity he usually only trusted on the floor, that there were only two ways to handle a real cutoff. Name it, or let quality decay while everyone called the delay manners. He had spent twenty minutes at the Mercer table today watching people stall because naming felt final. He had written the lesson down. He had even nodded when Claire read it back. It was still taking him this long to apply it to himself.

"I don't know," he said.

She nodded once. "Exactly."

He exhaled. Decision finally replacing restraint rather than decorating it.

"Tomorrow," he said. "After close. Come with me to Marina Bay."

Her expression changed by almost nothing. Not softness. Attention — the particular quality of attention she gave to things that mattered, the same expression she'd had reading the Mercer table, the same expression she'd had the first time she walked the Odette floor and understood what it actually required.

"Is this an operational invitation or an emotional one?"

"Yes."

That made her laugh — quick, quiet, impossible to mistake for dismissal.

Then she held his eyes one beat longer than comfort required.

"Tomorrow after close," she said. "Marina Bay."

"Yes."

"For dinner?"

"Probably too late for dinner."

"For whatever this is, then."

"Yes."

"Good," she said.

From the dining room, Claire's voice cut cleanly through the corridor wall.

"If either of you are about to become memorable in a hallway, don't. Table three needs spoons." Sofia closed her eyes briefly, amused. Then she picked up the tray stand again. "Duty calls," she said.

"Yes."

She was already turning back through the swing door when she glanced over her shoulder. "One more thing," she said.

"What?"

"Tell Nia she can stop insulting your calendar discipline for one evening."

"I'll consider it."

"No," Sofia said. "That would defeat the entire lesson."

Then she was gone.

After close, he walked alone toward the bay because the train felt impossible. Singapore at that hour refused to help. It did not turn itself soft for lovers or uncertain people. It remained glass, water, expensive light — a skyline too disciplined to lie on anyone's behalf, too clean to absorb whatever this was and give it back transformed. The waterfront was nearly empty, just a handful of people moving through with purpose, nobody lingering. The reflections of the buildings ran long and broken across the surface of the water. Not romantic. Just exact.

That was part of why he trusted it.

His phone buzzed. Nia.

Did you decide what you're doing with tomorrow night, or are you still curating uncertainty like a museum exhibit?

He looked out across the water and answered before he could improve the sentence into something less honest.

Decided.

Her reply came almost at once.

Finally. Pack the rest of your shirts and try not to ruin it by becoming articulate in advance. He laughed once, alone by the bay. The kind of laugh that happened because the alternative was something he didn't have vocabulary for yet.

He put the phone away and kept walking.

He was not calmer because he knew what Marina Bay would solve. He was calmer because he had finally stopped using uncertainty as manners. The distinction felt small from the outside and was enormous from the inside: one was waiting for clarity, the other was performing the waiting in order to avoid the clarity. He had been doing the second. He knew it. The week was specific now. It had always been specific — he had simply been narrating it as though it were still theoretical because theoretical was something you could stand outside of, examine without consequence, revise before committing.

Tomorrow was not theoretical.

When he reached the staff-housing door, he paused in the corridor. The building was quiet. Down the dim hallway, a strip of light showed under Sofia's door.

Still awake.

So was he.

Tomorrow now existed between them with the unnerving dignity of something properly confirmed — not the anxious weight of an unspoken thing, but the clean particular pressure of a decision that had stopped being a question. Like a service plan filed and understood: the structure real, the execution still ahead.

For the first time since Sydney had become a date instead of a rumor, Luca did not want more time in the abstract.

He wanted the next true hour.
