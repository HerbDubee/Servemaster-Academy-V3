How to Sell Wine Confidently Without Being Pushy

The language and technique that lets you recommend wine naturally, increase check averages, and leave guests feeling well looked after — not sold to.

ServeMaster Academy · 7 min read

As a fine-dining aficionado, I've seen too many great bottles go unsold because servers were nervous about recommending them. Not nervous about the wine itself — nervous about coming across as pushy. So they stay quiet, the guest picks the second-cheapest option by default, and everyone leaves a little disappointed.

The good news: confident, natural wine recommendation is a skill. It's learnable. And once you have it, it changes the way every table feels about their evening — and the way your check averages look at the end of the week. Here are the five techniques I teach in ServeMaster Academy.

Technique 1: Ask one question before you say a single wine name

The number one mistake I see is servers leading with a recommendation before they know anything about the guest. The guest's guard goes up the moment it feels like a pitch. One question changes that entirely.

My favourite opening:

"Are you in the mood for something red or white tonight — or are you open to a suggestion based on what you're ordering?"

That last clause is the key. It gives you permission to guide them, and it signals that your recommendation will be personal — tied to their food, their taste, their evening. Most guests say "sure, go ahead." That single yes is all you need.

Technique 2: Describe the experience, never the price

Price is the guest's job. Your job is to make them feel something about the wine before they look at the number. When you lead with price, you put the guest in accounting mode. When you lead with experience, they're already picturing the glass.

Instead of: "The Malbec is $68."

Try: "The Malbec is rich and fruit-forward — dark cherry, a little chocolate finish. It's exactly what I'd want alongside the short rib."

Now the guest is thinking about the short rib and dark cherry, not $68. The price hasn't changed. The feeling around it has.

Technique 3: Always offer three options at different levels

A single recommendation feels like pressure. Three options feel like service. I use a simple structure: approachable, elevated, special.

"If you want something easy-drinking and food-friendly, the Côtes du Rhône is excellent value right now. If you want to step it up, the Malbec pairs beautifully with what you've ordered. And if tonight's a celebration, the Barossa Shiraz is genuinely one of the best bottles we carry."

No prices mentioned. Guests who care about price will ask — and that's a perfectly comfortable conversation because they asked for it. Everyone else picks whatever sounded best, and it's almost never the cheapest one.

Technique 4: Own the "I don't know" moment

Every server hits a question they can't answer. A guest asks about a specific vintage, or a grape variety you haven't tried yet. The amateur response is to bluff or go vague. Both damage trust immediately.

The professional response:

"That's a great question — I haven't tried that one personally. Let me find out for you right now."

Then go ask someone who knows. Come back with the answer. What looks like a gap in knowledge becomes a demonstration that you take their question seriously. Guests trust servers who admit what they don't know far more than servers who fake it.

Technique 5: Check back two minutes after the pour

This is the technique most servers skip, and it's the one that builds the most loyalty. Once the wine is poured and you've moved on, come back — briefly — and check in.

"How is that drinking for you?"

Five words. Seven seconds. What it communicates is: I recommended that wine because I thought you'd enjoy it, and I genuinely want to know if I was right. That's not upselling. That's care. And guests who feel cared for spend more, tip better, and come back.

If they're not loving it, you have an opportunity to fix it — suggest something else, offer a taste of another option, involve the sommelier. A problem handled gracefully is worth ten smooth tables.

Put it into practice before your next shift

Reading these techniques is the start. The gap between reading and doing is where most servers stay stuck. The language has to be practiced out loud until it sounds natural — not like a script you memorised, but like something you'd actually say.
