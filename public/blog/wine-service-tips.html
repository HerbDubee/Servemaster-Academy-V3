Wine Service

How to Sell Wine Confidently Without Being Pushy

The language and technique that lets you recommend wine naturally, increase check averages, and leave guests feeling well looked after — not sold to.

ServeMaster Academy · 7 min read

There is a version of wine upselling that every guest can feel from a mile away. The server lists the most expensive bottles first. The suggestions arrive before anyone has looked at the menu. The recommendations come with price tags, not descriptions. Guests shut down, pick something safe, and move on.

Then there is the other version — where a knowledgeable server asks one or two smart questions, makes a genuine recommendation, and the table ends up with a bottle they love at a price point they feel good about. That version is not salesmanship. It is hospitality. And it is learnable.

Start with the question, not the bottle

The biggest mistake servers make with wine is leading with product. They point at the list or name a bottle before they know anything about what the table wants. The guest's guard goes up immediately.

Instead, start with a question that helps you understand their preferences:

  • "Are you thinking red or white this evening, or are you open to either?"
  • "Do you have a style you tend to enjoy — something lighter or more full-bodied?"
  • "Is this a special occasion, or are you looking for something easy-drinking?"

You are not running a survey. One good question is usually enough. The answer gives you everything you need to make a relevant suggestion — and it signals to the guest that you are listening, not just pitching.

The three-tier offer

When you make a recommendation, always give the guest a choice. A single suggestion feels like a push. Two or three options at different price points feel like service.

A natural structure:

"If you're looking for something approachable tonight, the Côtes du Rhône is really food-friendly and great value. If you want to step it up a little, the Malbec pairs beautifully with what you've ordered — it's a bit richer. And if you're in the mood to celebrate, the Barossa Shiraz is our best right now."

Notice what is absent: prices. You described the wine in terms of experience. Guests who want the price will ask. Those who just want guidance will pick the one that sounded best to them — and that is usually not the cheapest one.

Know three things about every wine on your list

You do not need to be a sommelier. You need to know three things about each wine you might recommend:

  • What it tastes like — one or two descriptors a non-expert will understand (fruit-forward, dry, earthy, crisp)
  • What food it goes with — especially from your current menu
  • Who it is for — the occasion, the preference, the guest type

If you can say "this is a dry, earthy Burgundy that pairs really well with the duck — guests who like Pinot Noir tend to love it," you sound knowledgeable, helpful, and confident. That is worth more than memorising every vintage year on the list.

Match the energy of the table

A table of four celebrating a promotion is not in the same headspace as a couple on a quiet Tuesday. Read the mood before you open your mouth. A celebration table often wants to be guided toward something special. A casual couple on a weeknight might want something unpretentious and easy.

Mirroring their energy — enthusiastic when they are, understated when they are — makes the recommendation feel personal rather than scripted.

Handle "we're fine with water" gracefully

Sometimes people genuinely do not want wine. The professional response is to accept that without drama, leave the door open, and move on.

"Of course — I'll leave the wine list in case anything catches your eye later."

You have not pressured them. You have not disappeared. And you have left an opening. Many tables who decline wine at the start of the meal order a bottle with their mains after seeing what everyone else ordered.

After the pour

Once the wine is open and poured, check back within a few minutes. Not to sell another bottle — but to make sure they like it. A simple "how is that drinking for you?" takes five seconds and signals that you care about the experience, not the sale.

Guests who feel looked after spend more. That is not a manipulation technique — it is a natural consequence of good hospitality.

Practice out loud

Reading about wine service and actually delivering a confident recommendation in a live dining room are very different things. The language needs to be practiced until it sounds natural, not rehearsed. Role-playing wine service scenarios — including when guests push back or ask hard questions — is the fastest way to close that gap.
